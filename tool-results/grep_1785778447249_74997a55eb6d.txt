10:The cosmology documents (00, 14, 15, 24) specified the cathedral: the three strata, the anchor, the realm ladder, the spirit veins, the courts of Heaven, the ancestral courts, the tribulation system. What they left unspecified was the congregation — the beings and forces that fill a mortal's actual religious life. The earth god in the small shrine at the village entrance. The river god who floods when offended. The ghosts that walk on the fifteenth of the seventh month. The fox-spirit in the mountain who seduces travelers. The demonic cultivator who harvests the dying. The underworld the dead are said to descend to. The dreams the ancestors send.
20:The folk religion of the Cangli Riverlands is not fantasy. The earth god is real. The river god is real. The ghosts of the unburied dead are real. The fox-spirit in the Cangwu foothills is real. The underworld is real. The dreams the ancestors send are real.
22:What is partial, what is myth, what is mortal projection, is the *interpretation*. The earth god is not a small bearded old man in a green robe who lives in the shrine — that is how mortal iconography depicts a being whose actual ontological category is "the consciousness of a spirit vein's surface trace." The underworld is not the ten courts of Yama with their mirror-pools and bridges of gold and silver — that is how Mahayana Buddhist popular eschatology depicts a bureaucratic processing center. The ancestors do not literally descend on the fifteenth of the seventh month — that is the calendar on which the boundary between the Mortal stratum and the bardo is thinnest, and on which a certain kind of perception is easiest.
24:This document's commitment is twofold. First: the phenomena are real. Second: the mortal accounts of them are interpretations, varying in accuracy, varying in elaboration, varying in moral coloring, but always interpretations of something that is actually there. The cultivator who reaches Core Formation and learns to perceive these beings directly inherits a responsibility that the mortal who merely believes in them does not: the responsibility to act on what is actually there rather than on what the iconography says.
32:A place-spirit is the **consciousness of a spirit vein's surface trace** when that trace is dense enough and venerated long enough. It is not a god, not a Precelestial official, not an ancestor, not a cultivator, not a beast, not a ghost. It is a sui generis ontological category: a self-sustaining qi-pattern that has acquired, through density and duration, the qualities of attention, memory, and will.
34:The vernacular calls them 公 (a respectful term for a low-ranking supernatural being): the earth god (土地公), the river god (河神), the city god (城隍). The cosmological term is place-spirit. Both are correct in their registers; the cosmological term is what a Core Formation cultivator uses when perceiving one directly, the vernacular term is what a mortal uses when burning incense at the shrine.
42:1. **A spirit vein's surface trace.** Per 14 §1.1, a spirit vein is a subterranean river of dense differentiated qi. Its surface trace is the plume of phase-aligned qi that diffuses upward through rock and soil to the surface. Most surface traces are too thin and too diffuse to support a place-spirit; they manifest only as the indicator ecologies 14 §1.3 describes (a grove of healthier trees, a spring that never fails). A place-spirit requires a surface trace dense enough to sustain a coherent qi-pattern — typically the trace above a shallow vein, or the trace at a vein's crown where it approaches the surface.
46:3. **Time.** The minimum duration is roughly three generations of continuous veneration — about ninety years of mortal time. Below that threshold, the pattern is present but not coherent; it is what a Qi Induction cultivator perceives as the "good feeling" of a shrine. Above that threshold, the pattern coheres into a being. Three generations is the floor; most place-spirits are far older. The earth god of Wang Family Bend has been venerated at the same shrine for nine generations (per 04 §5.3); the river god of the Cangli at the south-bank temple for at least seven.
56:- **Boundary enforcement.** A place-spirit can, within its jurisdiction, enforce a boundary against low-tier threats. A low-tier spirit beast (14 §2.2) that would range into the village is turned aside — not by force, but by the spirit's qi-pattern making the village's ambient qi feel *wrong-colored* to the beast, the way a smell makes a room unwelcoming. A wandering ghost (§3 below) is slowed, sometimes stopped, at the boundary. A Qi Condensation cultivator of malign intent feels, at the boundary, a faint pressure to turn back. None of these are hard barriers; a determined intruder of any tier above mortal passes through. The boundary is a modulation, not a wall.
60:A place-spirit's jurisdiction is **bounded to its place**. The earth god of Wang Family Bend has no authority in Li Family Creek, four li east. The river god of the Cangli has no authority over the Cangwu Mountains' streams. The city god of Cangli County Seat has no authority outside the city walls. This is not a politeness; it is ontological. The place-spirit's quasi-anchor *is* the place; outside the place, the spirit does not exist as a coherent being. A place-spirit that tries to extend its jurisdiction beyond its place simply dissipates — the qi-pattern that constitutes it unwinds when it leaves the surface trace that sustains it.
62:The relationship to the Courts of Heaven (15 §2) is **independence without sovereignty**. A place-spirit is not a court official. The courts did not appoint it; the courts cannot promote, demote, transfer, or remove it. The courts' records (生死簿) do not register place-spirits; they are not dead mortals, they are not cultivators, they are not beasts. They are a category the courts recognize but do not administer.
64:However: in regions where the courts' jurisdiction is firm (the maintained roads, the court-recognized territories), a place-spirit may be **deputized** by a local magistrate (15 §2.3) for a specific task — usually weather-modulation in a famine year, or boundary-enforcement against a specific threat. The deputyship is a contract: the spirit performs the task, the court pays in qi-authority (which the spirit can metabolize as concentrated veneration-equivalent), and the spirit's jurisdiction is formally recognized (which protects it from being absorbed by a neighboring spirit or destroyed by a careless cultivator). Most place-spirits accept deputyships readily. In regions where the courts' jurisdiction is thin (the frontiers, the wild margins, the places where the maintained roads do not reach), place-spirits survive on their own. They are the *de facto* supernatural authority in most of the Mortal stratum; a traveler in the deep country deals with place-spirits, not with the courts.
70:1. **Vein collapse.** If the spirit vein that sustains the place-spirit fails — through over-tapping (14 §1.4), through enemy action, through geological accident — the surface trace disperses, the qi-pattern that constitutes the spirit unwinds, and the spirit ends. This is the most common cause of place-spirit death in regions subject to sect cultivation. A sect that over-taps a vein kills, often unknowingly, the place-spirit above it. The mortal community that depended on the spirit experiences the death as the slow fading of the shrine's "feel" — incense seems less effective, prayers seem unheard, the village's luck goes sour. The mortals blame themselves, or the spirit, or the weather. They are wrong about all three.
72:2. **Veneration cessation.** If the community that venerated the place-spirit disperses — through famine, war, plague, migration — the veneration-qi input stops. The qi-pattern that constitutes the spirit, no longer fed, unwinds over years. This is a slow death; a place-spirit whose community abandons it persists for a generation or two as a fading echo, then ends. The ruins of a village with an unattended shrine often harbor such fading echoes. A Qi Induction cultivator passing through perceives them as a thin, melancholy presence — the spirit's last pattern, repeating without coherence.
74:3. **Destruction of the anchor-object.** A place-spirit's quasi-anchor, unlike a true anchor, has a physical seat — the shrine's main image (the small carved figure, the painted tablet, the inscribed stone) that the community's veneration has, over generations, invested with the spirit's pattern. This is the spirit's *anchor-object*. Destroying the anchor-object does not, by itself, kill the spirit — the spirit's pattern is in the place, not in the object — but it severs the spirit's primary channel of veneration-input and shatters the coherence of the pattern. A spirit whose anchor-object is destroyed enters a confused, diminished state from which it recovers only if the community rebuilds the object quickly (within a season) and resumes veneration. If the community does not — if the destruction was deliberate, by a hostile cultivator or a rival sect — the spirit usually unwinds within a year.
76:This third vulnerability is what makes place-spirits politically fragile. A cultivator who wishes to displace a place-spirit (to clear a site for sect construction, to free a vein for direct tapping, to remove a community's supernatural patron) does not need to destroy the vein or the community; destroying the anchor-object and preventing its reconstruction suffices. This is why place-spirits are protective of their shrines and why communities are protective of their shrines' images. The image is not the spirit; the image is the spirit's handle.
82:To a Qi Induction cultivator, the place-spirit is perceived as a thickening of the ambient qi — the surface trace becomes legible as a coherent texture rather than a background diffusion. The cultivator can feel the spirit's attention as a pressure, like the pressure of being watched but with a quality (color, weight, temperature) that the cultivator can begin to describe. They cannot yet perceive the spirit as a being with a shape and a voice.
84:To a Qi Condensation cultivator, the place-spirit is legible as a presence — a qi-pattern with coherence, memory, and will. The cultivator can address it; the spirit can respond (not in words, but in modulations of the ambient qi that the cultivator can read). This is the conversation a village priest believes he is having; the Qi Condensation cultivator is the first being who is actually having it.
86:To a Core Formation cultivator, the place-spirit is fully legible. The cultivator perceives the spirit's quasi-anchor directly (per 00 §2 — Core Formation is the threshold of anchor-perception), reads its coherence, its age, its veneration-input, its jurisdiction's boundaries. The cultivator can also perceive the vein beneath and the community around, and can see how the three sustain each other. A Core Formation cultivator visiting a village for the first time typically perceives the place-spirit within the first hour and may, if courteous, present themselves at the shrine with a small offering — a courtesy the spirit registers and the community does not understand.
90:The earth god of Wang Family Bend is named, in the village's tradition, Wang Tugeng — a name the village gave the spirit three generations ago, when an elder dreamed of an old man in a green robe who said he had been the lineage's first tenant farmer in the founder's time. The dream was a real perception (per §7 below); the spirit did not, in fact, claim to be Wang Tianzu's tenant farmer. The spirit's quasi-anchor is older than the Wang lineage's settlement; it cohered above a minor wood-phase vein's surface trace sometime in the centuries before the Wangs arrived, sustained by the veneration of an earlier community whose name is forgotten. The Wangs adopted the spirit when they settled the bend; the spirit adopted the Wangs in return.
92:The spirit is, in personality, cautious and conservative. Nine generations of veneration have made it coherent and reasonably powerful for a village-scale place-spirit, but the long memory of the bend's repeated floods (most recently forty years ago, per 24 §4.4) has made it wary. It nudges the village's luck in small ways — a child's fever breaking an hour early, a fox that turns away from the henhouse — but it does not intervene in the larger patterns of the bend's life. The village perceives this as the spirit being "old and tired"; the spirit perceives this as the spirit being prudent. The Azure Sword Sect's Core Formation sect master, Leng Wushuang, has visited the shrine once, sixty years ago, and left a small offering; the spirit remembers the visit and speaks of it (to the rare Qi Condensation cultivator who asks) as the day "the tall one with the cold eyes came and was polite."
108:The city god is, in personality, **bureaucratic and deputized**. Of the three named place-spirits here, it is the only one with a formal deputyship from the local court magistrate (a Tribulation Crossing cultivator in the courts' Records department, who administers the Jiangnan region from a Precelestial office). The deputyship requires the city god to register, in its own quasi-anchor's memory, every death within its jurisdiction — name, date, cause — and to relay the register to the courts' Records department via a periodic rite performed by the county's Daoist priest. This is the place-spirit's most important function for the courts: it is the **local recorder of the dead**, the first link in the chain by which the dead are processed (see §6 below). The city god does not adjudicate; it records. Its register is duplicated in the courts'生死簿; discrepancies between the two are investigated, and a city god caught falsifying its register is destroyed (its anchor-object shattered, its pattern unwound). This has happened twice in the Cangli County Seat's history, in both cases for bribery by lineages seeking to conceal a murder.
118:The bardo state is bounded. Per 00 §2, the bardo is "an intermediate state between death and rebirth that lasts a bounded, lawful time." The typical window is forty-nine days (the genre's traditional period, ratified here as the lawful default). Within that window, the anchor is processed — retrieved by a lineage rite (if the deceased was a great cultivator and the lineage is competent), retrieved by a sect rite (if the deceased was a sect member and the sect is motivated), or processed by the underworld (§6 below) for rebirth. A ghost is what happens when the anchor is **not** processed within the window. The anchor, at the window's end, has three lawful paths: rebirth (the default), final death (寂滅 — the anchor disperses, the being ends), or — if the anchor's karmic trace contains an attachment of sufficient strength — persistence in bardo, anchored to a Mortal place, becoming a ghost.
128:3. **Failure of normal processing.** The lineage's rites did not retrieve the anchor (no lineage, no rites, or rites improperly performed). The sect's rites did not retrieve it (no sect, or sect unaware). The underworld's processing did not retrieve it — usually because the death was not registered (a murder concealed, a death in a remote place, a death the city god's register missed). The bardo window closes with the anchor unprocessed; the attachment and the anchor-point hold it; the ghost forms.
143:- **Manifest as a qi-presence.** A ghost with sufficient qi-density (sustained by years of persistence and amplified by mortal fear, which is itself a qi-input) can manifest as a perceptible qi-presence — a coldness, a heaviness, a sound (footsteps, a voice, a name called), a shape (a figure, a face, a hand). The manifestation is not the ghost's body; the ghost has no body. The manifestation is the ghost's anchor's qi-projection, shaped by the ghost's will and colored by its attachment. A mortal perceives the manifestation as a haunting; a Qi Induction cultivator perceives it as a coherent qi-presence with a recognizable signature; a Qi Condensation cultivator can read the attachment directly in the signature's color.
145:- **Influence the heart-mind of the living.** A ghost can, by projecting its qi-presence into the qi-field of a sleeping or distracted mortal, influence the mortal's heart-mind (心). A grief-ghost projects sorrow; a hatred-ghost projects anger; an oath-ghost projects urgency; a love-ghost projects longing. The influence is subtle — a mood that seems to come from nowhere, a thought that seems the mortal's own. A mortal with strong heart-mind coherence (a Qi Condensation cultivator, a disciplined meditator, a person of profound stability) resists easily; a mortal with weak coherence (the exhausted, the grieving, the sick, the drunk) is susceptible. This is the mechanism behind the mortal world's "ghost-sickness" (14 §6.3) — a chronic low-grade malaise caused by a ghost's persistent influence on a susceptible mortal's heart-mind.
153:An **ancestor** is an anchor that has been retrieved from bardo and lifted into the Precelestial stratum (15 §3), sustained by the lineage's veneration and the ancestral court's qi-authority. It is disembodied in the same sense (no body), but it is not place-bound (it resides in the ancestral court, a Precelestial zone), it is not attachment-driven (it persists by the lineage's rite, not by an unresolved attachment), and it is intended to be permanent (it persists as long as the court and the veneration sustain it). The mortal world conflates the two — the village's "ancestors" include both the lineage-hall's venerated dead (some of whom may be true ancestors in a cultivation lineage, most of whom are merely remembered) and the graveyard's ghosts (some of whom may persist as actual ghosts, most of whom have long since reincarnated). The cosmology distinguishes sharply. A ghost is what happens when the normal system fails; an ancestor is what happens when the normal system succeeds at a high enough level.
161:2. **Forcibly disperse the anchor-fragment.** A Core Formation cultivator (the threshold of anchor-perception, per 00 §2) can perceive a ghost's anchor directly and can, with the right technique, disperse the anchor-fragment that constitutes the ghost's persistence in bardo. This is **not** final death (寂滅) in the full sense — the ghost's anchor was already severed from the body at the original death; what is dispersed is the fragment that persisted as the ghost. The dispersal ends the ghost; the being's karmic trace, already recorded in the courts' register, is not erased. The being does not reincarnate from this fragment; the fragment ends. Whether the being's broader anchor (the original law-pattern) persists in some other form is a question the cosmology does not answer; the cosmology specifies only that the ghost ends.
163:The first method is preferred by every tradition — Buddhist, Daoist, lineage, sect. The second is a last resort, used when the ghost is harmful and the attachment cannot be resolved (a hatred-ghost whose killer is dead and whose hatred persists; an oath-ghost whose oath is impossible to fulfill). The second is criminal under the courts' jurisdiction if performed without authorization — dispersing an anchor-fragment without first attempting resolution is treated as a form of unlawful killing, and a cultivator who does it carelessly faces tribulation (15 §6.2, "killing a being above your realm" can apply if the ghost's original being was of higher station than the cultivator). The Azure Sword Sect's punishment hall has a standing commission for ghost-dispersal in the Cangwu foothills, and the commission's rules require documented attempts at resolution before dispersal is authorized.
173:The mortal rites of the festival — the offerings at the shrine, the lineage rite for unburied or unremembered dead, the burning of paper money and paper goods — are **functional**. The offerings are qi-inputs (the food's qi, the incense's qi, the paper-goods' burning-qi) directed at the bardo-boundary, where they are absorbed by whatever anchors are present. The lineage rite for unremembered dead is a controlled bardo-feeding: it provides enough qi-density to fading anchors (the unremembered, the unvenerated, the orphans) to allow them to either resolve their attachment (with the lineage's farewell) or to be processed by the underworld (which uses the festival's thinness as an efficient processing window — see §6 below). The burning of paper money is, metaphysically, the burning of qi-charged paper for the qi-release; the paper-money's "value" in the underworld is a mortal projection onto a real qi-input. The festival is **inauspicious for travel** because the bardo-boundary is thin everywhere, and a traveler moving through unfamiliar places encounters unfamiliar ghosts at near-full manifestation. The mortal advice to stay home is sound advice.
179:To a Qi Induction cultivator, a ghost is perceived as a **cold spot** in the ambient qi — a region where the qi is denser, slower, and yin-dominant in a way that does not match the local qi-climate. The cold spot has a shape (often the shape of the anchor-point — a room, a crossroads, a stretch of riverbank) and a movement (slow, often circular, often returning to a center). The cultivator can feel the ghost's presence but cannot read its attachment.
181:To a Qi Condensation cultivator, the ghost is legible as a coherent being — an anchor-fragment with a signature, an attachment, a will. The cultivator can read the attachment's type (grief, hatred, oath, love) in the signature's color, and can address the ghost directly. This is the conversation a Daoist priest believes he is having when performing an exorcism; the Qi Condensation cultivator is the first being who is actually having it. Most ghosts, addressed directly by a Qi Condensation cultivator, will speak (in qi-modulations the cultivator can read) about their attachment and what would resolve it. A cultivator who listens and acts on what they hear can lay a ghost to rest without combat. A cultivator who does not listen — who treats the ghost as a thing to be dispersed — must resort to the second method (§3.6), which is harder, slower, and riskier.
183:To a Core Formation cultivator, the ghost is fully legible — the anchor-fragment perceived directly, its coherence read, its attachment read, its history (the being's life, death, and persistence) readable in the karmic trace. The Core Formation cultivator can also perceive the ghost's relationship to the courts' register — whether the death was recorded, whether the ghost is known to the underworld, whether dispersal would be lawful. A Core Formation cultivator who disperses a ghost without consulting the register risks the courts' attention.
193:This distinguishes the yao sharply from a spirit beast. Per 14 §2, a spirit beast is an animal that has absorbed ambient qi and developed a crude internal circulation. The four tiers (low, mid, high, peak) describe the depth of that qi-cultivation. But a spirit beast, even at peak tier, has only body and qi — it has no anchor. Its persistence is the persistence of an animal; its death is the death of an animal; it does not bear karmic trace; it does not reincarnate. A yao is what happens when a peak-tier spirit beast's inner core (內丹, per 14 §2.2) — the proto-anchor that the peak-tier beast begins to form — **completes its inscription** and becomes a true anchor. This is rare. Most peak-tier beasts never complete the transition; their inner core remains a proto-anchor, a dense qi-structure that resembles an anchor but lacks an anchor's noncopyability, karmic-trace capacity, and persistence across death. The beast dies; the proto-anchor disperses; the being ends.
195:The peak-tier beast that completes the transition becomes a yao. The yao has, in addition to its animal body and qi, a true anchor — and with the anchor comes everything the anchor implies: karmic trace, persistence across death, the bardo state, the possibility of rebirth, the possibility of final death (寂滅). The yao is, in this sense, the only nonhuman being in the Mortal stratum that is **metaphysically equivalent to a human**. It is not human; it does not have a human body or human cognition or human culture. But it has an anchor, and the anchor is the seat of personhood in this cosmology. This is why the genre treats yao as persons — fox-spirits who seduce, snake-demons who scheme, tree-spirits who protect groves — and not as beasts. The cosmology agrees. A yao is a person, in the metaphysical sense, with an animal body and an animal's ecology. The moral and lawful implications are significant: a yao can be wronged (its anchor can be dispersed unlawfully); a yao can bear karma (its acts inscribe trace on its anchor); a yao can be saved (its anchor can be retrieved from bardo by a lineage or sect rite, if the yao had such ties); a yao can be reborn.
206:### 4.3 The cultivation path
208:A yao's cultivation path is **harder than a human's** in two ways and different in a third.
210:1. **The yao begins with an animal body and an animal's cognition.** A human cultivator begins with a body evolved for tool-use, social cognition, and language; the human's cultivation builds on these. A yao begins with a body evolved for its species' niche — a fox's body for stealth and cunning, a snake's for ambush and constriction, a tree's for rooted patience. The yao's cultivation must, first, develop the cognitive and somatic capacities that a human begins with. This is the yao's first additional burden.
212:2. **The yao must take human form.** The human form is, in this cosmology, the architecture best suited to anchor-expression — the body's meridian system, the brain's cognitive complexity, the vocal apparatus's capacity for speech and oath. A yao that wishes to advance past the most basic anchor-integration must take stable human form. The peak-tier beast's imperfect shapeshifting (the tail that won't vanish, the eyes that stay beast-shaped) becomes, for the yao, a cultivation practice — the yao refines its shapeshifting until the human form is stable, fluent, and indistinguishable (to mortal perception) from a human's. This is the genre's "beast-takes-human-form" trope, and it is, in this cosmology, a metaphysical necessity: the human form is the architecture the anchor can most fully express through.
222:A **spirit beast** (14 §2) is an animal with qi. It has body and qi; it has no anchor. It lives an animal's life, dies an animal's death, and (if it has lived long enough over a spirit vein) may have advanced through the four tiers. Its inner core (at peak tier) is a proto-anchor, not a true anchor.
226:The confusion arises because the mortal world calls both "demons" (妖, the same character used for the yao) and treats both as threats. The cosmology distinguishes sharply. A mid-tier spirit tiger that preys on livestock is a beast problem, solvable by hunting. A tiger-yao that preys on livestock is a person committing a crime, solvable by negotiation, treaty, or (if the yao refuses to cease) lawful killing — and lawful killing of a yao is, under most jurisdictions, treated as the killing of a person, with the karmic and lawful consequences that entails (24 §2.1, 15 §6.2). A Qi Condensation cultivator hunting a mid-tier spirit wolf is hunting an animal; the same cultivator hunting a wolf-yao is killing a person. The two acts feel similar (the cultivator tracks, fights, kills a wolf-shaped being) but are metaphysically and lawfully distinct. The cultivator who cannot tell the difference — who kills a yao thinking it a spirit beast — bears the karmic trace of the killing nonetheless, and faces the tribulation that killing a being of equivalent station triggers (15 §6.2). This is why sects train their hunting disciples to distinguish spirit beasts from yao, and why a hunting disciple who reports a yao-kill is subject to a sect investigation.
230:The fox-spirit of the Cangwu foothills is named, in the market town's tradition, Lady Bai — a name she did not give and does not use. She is a red fox (Vulpes vulpes) who achieved peak-tier spirit beast status roughly four hundred years ago, in the eastern Cangwu range, over a minor wood-phase vein. She completed the anchor-transition roughly two hundred and fifty years ago, surviving a heart-demon tribulation whose central attachment was the memory of her first mate, killed by a hunter in her second century. She took stable human form — a young woman of about twenty, with dark hair, dark eyes, and (in moments of inattention) a faint reddish cast to her shadow — roughly two hundred years ago.
232:Lady Bai's cultivation is, by human reckoning, late Foundation Establishment — she has integrated body, qi, and anchor into a coherent system, and her human form is fluent. She has not crossed Core Formation; the form-tribulation at Core Formation is the next test, and she has been preparing for it for thirty years. Her ecology is the ecology of a fox — she hunts small game in the eastern Cangwu, she avoids the larger predators (the Cangwu Sect's mid-tier beasts), she mates (occasionally, with mortal foxes, producing offspring that are mortal with slight qi-affinity), she dens in a copse of old-growth oak above the wood-phase vein that sustained her peak-tier cultivation.
234:Her relationship with mortals is the genre's classic fox-spirit relationship. She is curious about humans (a yao's cognition includes the curiosity its anchor permits); she occasionally takes human form and walks in the market town (Qinghe Market Town, 15 li east of Wang Family Bend) to listen to gossip and buy fruit (she pays with copper she has acquired honestly, by healing a farmer's child in exchange for the coins — a transaction neither party fully understands); she has, twice in the last century, taken a mortal lover (in both cases a young scholar; in both cases the relationship ended when the scholar aged past her ability to maintain the pretense, and she left, leaving behind a grief that is now part of her karmic trace). She has never harmed a mortal unprovoked. She is, by any reasonable measure, a good-faith resident of the Cangwu foothills.
236:The mortal world's tradition of fox-spirits as seducers and tricksters is a partial truth derived from cases like Lady Bai's: yao-foxes do take human form, do walk among humans, do occasionally form attachments. The tradition's elaboration — that fox-spirits steal men's semen to fuel their cultivation, that they are uniformly malicious, that they can be exorcised by Daoist priests — is mortal projection. Lady Bai's cultivation is sustained by the wood-phase vein and her own practice, not by any human substance. She is not malicious. A Daoist priest's exorcism has no effect on her (she has an anchor; an exorcism designed to disperse a ghost's anchor-fragment does not work on a true anchor integrated with a living body). The Azure Sword Sect knows of her: the previous sect master (who pursued Zhou Wugui, §5.4 below) consulted her once, seventy years ago, on the question of whether a particular Cangwu foothills spirit beast was a yao or not (it was not; he let it live). The current sect master, Leng Wushuang, has not contacted her. The Cangwu Sect (the small local sect, 24 §4.3) treats her as a known presence in their territory and leaves her alone, because she is more powerful than their sect master and because she has never caused them trouble. The mortal world mostly does not know she exists; the few who have met her in human form remember a young woman from the market town whom they cannot quite place.
240:The snake-demon of the Cangwu foothills is named, in the local tradition, the Black Creek Serpent — a name the yao itself does not use (it does not speak, having never developed the capacity, and does not take human form). It is a king cobra (Ophiophagus hannah) that achieved peak-tier spirit beast status roughly six hundred years ago in the western Cangwu, over a water-phase vein. It attempted the anchor-transition three hundred years ago and survived — barely — a heart-demon tribulation whose central attachment was the memory of a predator (a Cangwu Sect Core Formation cultivator, two centuries dead, who had killed its first mate). The yao's anchor is true; its cognition is not. The transition completed the anchor but did not complete the cognitive development that usually accompanies it.
242:The Black Creek Serpent is, in consequence, a yao with the cognition of a peak-tier spirit beast and the anchor of a Foundation Establishment-equivalent being. It does not take human form (it has not developed the cognitive capacity for the practice); it does not speak; it does not negotiate. It hunts (other snakes, small mammals, occasionally a mid-tier spirit beast it can overpower), it defends its territory (a five-li stretch of the western Cangwu's black-creek valley), and it kills humans who enter its territory without the sense to retreat. It has killed eleven humans in the last century — mostly charcoal burners and herb-gatherers who wandered into its valley.
244:The Cangwu Sect treats it as a problem. The Azure Sword Sect treats it as a Cangwu Sect problem. The immortal alliance's regional office (12 §6) has, twice, asked the Azure Sword Sect to disperse it; the Azure Sword Sect has, twice, declined, on the grounds that the yao has an anchor (and is therefore a person under alliance law) and that dispersal would be unlawful without a documented attempt at resolution. The documented attempts at resolution have failed (the yao does not negotiate; the territory cannot be ceded because mortal charcoal burners depend on it). The situation is, at present, a slow-burning problem that no one has the will or the resources to solve. A mortal who enters the western Cangwu's black-creek valley and does not return is mourned by their family and not avenged by the sects. This is the cultivation world's moral failure at small scale, and it is common.
248:The thousand-year tree-spirit of the eastern Cangwu is named, in the lineage traditions of three Cangwu-village lineages, Old Cypress — a name the yao accepts (it perceives the name, in the qi-modulations of the lineages' veneration, as a qi-input that feeds its anchor). It is a cypress tree (Platycladus orientalis) that achieved peak-tier spirit beast status roughly eight hundred years ago, over a wood-phase vein that surfaces beneath its roots. It completed the anchor-transition roughly five hundred years ago, surviving a heart-demon tribulation whose central attachment was — and remains — the memory of the grove it had grown in, which was logged by a mortal lineage four hundred years before its transition. The grove's loss is the yao's defining attachment; it has spent the last five centuries protecting the remaining cypress grove of which it is the oldest tree.
252:Its cultivation is, by human reckoning, late Foundation Establishment, sustained by the wood-phase vein and by the veneration of the three Cangwu-village lineages that have, for centuries, treated the grove as sacred. The veneration-qi feeds its anchor the way veneration-qi feeds a place-spirit (§2.2 above); Old Cypress is, in this sense, both a yao and the place-spirit of its grove. The cosmology permits this overlap; the two categories are not mutually exclusive when a yao's body is the place.
254:Old Cypress protects its grove. A mortal who cuts a cypress in the grove dies — the yao, perceiving the cut through its root-network, routes a surge of wood-phase qi through the soil that stops the mortal's heart (a mortal's heart is a delicate thing; a wood-phase qi-surge through the earth is enough). A mortal who venerates at the grove's central tree is blessed — the yao routes a minor wood-phase qi-nudge that strengthens the mortal's constitution for a season. A Qi Condensation cultivator who enters the grove with hostile intent is challenged — the yao's roots shift, the canopy thickens, the ambient qi becomes heavy and difficult. A Foundation Establishment cultivator who enters the grove with hostile intent is fought — the yao can project its anchor's qi as a quasi-domain (not a true Spirit Severance domain, but a localized qi-authority within its grove), and within the grove it is the equal of a mid-Foundation Establishment human cultivator.
256:Old Cypress has, in its five centuries as a yao, killed thirty-two mortals (all cypress-cutters) and zero cultivators (the sects know the grove is protected and do not contest it). The Cangwu Sect treats it with wary respect; the Azure Sword Sect treats it as a regional asset (a Foundation Establishment yao protecting a wood-phase vein is a stabilizing presence); the three Cangwu-village lineages venerate it. Old Cypress remembers every name venerated at its central tree; its anchor's karmic trace is a record of five centuries of grove-life, and the record is the yao's most valued possession.
262:To a Qi Induction cultivator, a yao in animal form is perceived as a peak-tier spirit beast with an anomalous qi-pattern — the proto-anchor's qi-density is present, but there is an additional quality, a depth, that the proto-anchor does not have. The cultivator cannot read the additional quality; they perceive it as "this beast is different in a way I cannot describe." This perception is the first hint a cultivator gets that they are dealing with a yao and not a spirit beast.
264:To a Qi Condensation cultivator, the yao's anchor is legible as a presence — not as a fully perceived anchor (that requires Core Formation), but as a coherent qi-presence with a quality the cultivator recognizes as anchor-like. A Qi Condensation cultivator who has met a yao before can identify one reliably; a Qi Condensation cultivator encountering a yao for the first time often mistakes it for an unusually powerful peak-tier beast, with sometimes disastrous consequences.
266:To a Core Formation cultivator, the yao's anchor is fully legible. The cultivator perceives the anchor's coherence, its karmic trace, its attachments, its cultivation-state. The cultivator can address the yao directly (in qi-modulations the yao can read), and the yao can respond. This is the conversation the mortal tradition of "negotiating with the demon" describes; the Core Formation cultivator is the first human who can actually have it. A Core Formation cultivator meeting Lady Bai for the first time typically perceives, within the first minute, a young woman whose anchor is a fox's, whose karmic trace includes two centuries of curiosity and two griefs, and whose cultivation is at the threshold of Core Formation. The cultivator's appropriate response is courtesy; most Core Formation cultivators, meeting Lady Bai, extend it.
270:## 5. Demonic cultivators (魔修)
274:A demonic cultivator is **a cultivator who uses other beings' anchors as fuel**. The ontological category is "human cultivator" — body, qi, anchor, the standard triad. What makes the demonic cultivator demonic is not a different ontology; it is a specific practice that the cosmology, the courts, the alliance, and every sect and lineage treat as the one true evil.
276:The evil is metaphysical, not moral. The cosmology's anchor (24 §2.2) is noncopyable; it is the being's persistent law-pattern; it is what makes the being a being rather than a process. Final death (寂滅, per 00 §2) is the dispersion of the anchor — the one true end, the unrecoverable. The cosmos permits final death in specific lawful circumstances: high-realm conflict, tribulation failure, the natural expiry of the bardo window. These are lawful because they are either the being's own act (the cultivator who pushes a tribulation past their capacity), a contest between peers, or the cosmos's own boundary-enforcement.
278:The demonic cultivator's act is **the deliberate dispersion of another being's anchor for the cultivator's own power**. This is the cosmology's one true evil because it is the one act that produces **irreversible metaphysical destruction** for the cultivator's benefit. Killing a mortal inscribes a karmic trace (24 §2.1), but the mortal's anchor persists in bardo and enters rebirth — the being continues. Killing a cultivator inscribes a heavier trace, but the cultivator's anchor can be retrieved, can persist in an ancestral court, can be reborn. Even unlawful killing leaves the anchor intact; the being's persistence is not destroyed, only the body is. The demonic cultivator destroys the persistence: the harvested anchor's law-pattern is unwound, its karmic trace is erased, the being's continuity is ended. There is no rebirth from a dispersed anchor. There is no ancestral court. There is no future life, no future meeting, no future redemption. The being is gone, finally and irreversibly, and the cultivator has done it for power.
280:This is the one act that the cosmos cannot repair. Tribulation can be retaken. Karma can be processed. Deviation can be treated. Even final death by tribulation is, in a sense, the cosmos's lawful boundary-enforcement — the cultivator took a risk and the risk did not pay. The demonic cultivator's act is none of these. It is the unilateral, irreversible, unauthorized destruction of a being's persistence for the destroyer's gain. The courts have no record that restores it. The ancestral courts have no anchor to retrieve. The being is gone, and the cultivator who did it has the being's power.
