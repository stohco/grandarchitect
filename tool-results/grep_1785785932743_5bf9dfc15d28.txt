**Wang Meili (王美麗)** — the protagonist's cousin (father's brother's daughter), twenty-two at the protagonist's arrival, early Qi Condensation, healer-track. She entered the sect at fourteen against her father's wishes (her father, the protagonist's uncle Wang Shouye, distrusts Old Chen and by extension all retired cultivators; he sent Meili to the Cangwu Sect because it was a sect, which is to say an institution, which he understands, rather than a hermit, which he does not). She is small, watchful, precise, with calloused hands from the herb garden and a quiet steadiness the sect's other outer disciples find either reassuring or unnerving depending on their own temperaments. She has, by the time the protagonist arrives, been the sect's effective healer for two years — the previous healer, an old inner disciple named Lin Shu, died of a stroke the previous winter and Wu Changqing has not replaced her. Meili's practice is wood-phase (good for healing, good for growth) with a water-phase secondary. She is the protagonist's first ally in the sect and, eventually (doc 26 §9.1), the lineage bridge.
**Yan Boyan (嚴伯言)** — twenty-eight, late Qi Condensation, the protagonist's first rival in the sect. Yan is the son of the headman of Cangwu Sect's largest tenant village, sent to the sect at eight on a sect-recruitment tour. He is broad-shouldered, handsome in a hard-jawed way, meticulous about his appearance, and ambitious in a way that has curdled over twenty years of being the most talented disciple in a sect too small to reward him. He attempted Foundation Establishment at twenty-five and failed — not as catastrophically as Wu Changqing (his meridians are intact) but badly enough that he has not tried again. He believes (without proof) that Wu Changqing has held back a key technique from him. He believes (with some proof) that the Azure Sword Sect would treat him better. He is the leader of what the inner disciples quietly call the **Northern Faction** — those who favor accepting Azure Sword Sect patronage. When the protagonist arrives — a Foundation Establishment cultivator who will, by sect custom, be ranked above Yan despite having done nothing for the sect — Yan's resentment finds a new object. The rivalry is professional at first (spirit-stone allocations, mission assignments, scripture-hall access), then personal, then (by Act 3, depending on player choices) factional.
**Zhao Wujiu (趙五九)** — twenty-four, mid Qi Condensation, the protagonist's first friend in the sect. "Wujiu" is a childhood name (Fifth-Nine, from his birth order in his father's lineage and his mother's) that he never replaced with a courtesy name because, he says, he likes being numbered. He is tall, soft-voiced, ugly in a kind way (a long nose, a crooked smile), and the son of a herdboy from a tenant village. He entered the sect at twelve (a late recruit — the sect found him when he was helping drive cattle past the Stone Gate and a senior disciple noticed he could perceive the creek's qi without instruction). His practice is earth-phase, slow and steady, and his cultivation has been the slowest in his cohort — he is twenty-four and only mid Qi Condensation, where Yan Boyan is twenty-eight and late Qi Condensation. Zhao Wujiu does not mind. He likes the sect. He likes the routine. He likes Wu Changqing. He will become the protagonist's first friend in the sect because he is the only inner disciple who, on the protagonist's first day, talks to the protagonist without calculating.
**Su Wenli (蘇文理)** — thirty-one, Qi Condensation peak, the betrayer. Su is the son of a county-school teacher from Cangli County Seat who paid the sect a silver fee to take his son as an outer disciple at fifteen (a practice the sect accepts when the treasury is low, which is most years). Su is intelligent, well-read, articulate, and possessed of a self-regard that he has learned to disguise as humility. He is the sect's effective Scripture Hall steward (the actual hall head, an elderly inner disciple named Old Liu, is in early-stage cognitive decline and lets Su run the hall). Su is also, as the protagonist will discover over Act 2, in quiet correspondence with Elder Rong Zhaoyuan of the Azure Sword Sect (the Merit Hall head and leader of the Azure Sword expansionists, §2.5). The correspondence began four years ago, after the Azure Sword Sect's last tribute-collection visit, when Rong Zhaoyuan complimented Su on a memo he had written about the Cangwu Sect's vein output. Su does not think of himself as a traitor. He thinks of himself as a realist. The Cangwu Sect is dying; the Azure Sword Sect is the future; the protagonist's arrival is a complication in an arrangement he has spent four years building. He will betray the sect in Act 2 by leaking the protagonist's Foundation Establishment status and the vein's deep-tap location to Rong Zhaoyuan — information that accelerates the Azure Sword Sect's formal claim at session ~300.
**Old Liu (劉師兄, Liu Shixiong)** — forty-seven, mid Qi Condensation, the sect's Scripture Hall head in name and the outer disciples' de facto father in practice. Old Liu entered the sect at seven (the youngest recruit in sect history) and has not left the valley in thirty-eight years. He is short, round, perpetually amused, and unable to read anything more complex than the sect's basic manuals — which is why he let Su Wenli take over the Scripture Hall's administrative work. He teaches the outer disciples' morning scripture class himself (from memory; he has the foundational manual memorized in its entirety, with all Wu Changqing's annotations) and he is the one who, when an outer disciple is crying in the dormitory at two in the morning, gets up and makes them tea. He is, in his way, the sect's actual heart. He will not betray the sect, will not rise in it, and will die (in Act 3, of a stroke, at sixty-one, in the Scripture Hall with a cup of tea in his hand) having never perceived his own anchor.
**Ma Yuening (馬月寧)** — nineteen, early Qi Condensation (broke through three months before the protagonist's arrival; the breakthrough was the sect's social event of the year), the youngest inner disciple. She is from a fishing family on the Eastern Sea coast — recruited on the same tour that produced Yan Boyan — and she is, in temperament, the opposite of Yan: open, excitable, generous with her opinions, prone to crushes (on Yan at sixteen, on Su at eighteen, on Zhao Wujiu at nineteen — the last of which Zhao has not noticed). Her practice is water-phase (matching the vein), and she is the most talented pure cultivator the sect has produced in two generations. Wu Changqing, privately, believes she might be the one — the disciple who reaches Foundation Establishment legitimately. He has not told her this; he is afraid of putting the same pressure on her that broke him.
**The Cao Twins — Cao Shan (曹山) and Cao Hai (曹海)** — both thirty, late Qi Condensation, sons of a deceased inner disciple (Cao Ping, who died in a beast hunt twelve years ago) and raised in the sect. They are not identical — Cao Shan is the older by half an hour, taller, slower, kinder; Cao Hai is shorter, quicker, sharper — but they have spent thirty years finishing each other's sentences and the sect has stopped trying to tell them apart except by height. They are Yan Boyan's wingmen in the Northern Faction, more out of lifelong habit than conviction. They will, if the protagonist befriends Yan, follow Yan; if the protagonist befriends them, they will (reluctantly, then with relief) follow the protagonist. They are the sect's swing vote in any inner-disciple dispute.
**Peng Li (彭力)** — thirty-five, mid Qi Condensation, the sect's woodworker and the dormitory's eldest occupant. He is the only disciple who entered the sect as an adult (he was a carpenter in Qinghe Market Town; he perceived qi at twenty-eight after a long illness and walked to the Stone Gate on foot; Wu Changqing admitted him on the spot). He is quiet, slow-speaking, large-handed, and the only disciple in the sect who can repair a warded door or rebuild a qi-channel in the spirit-vein tap. He is indispensable and knows it. He is also, like Zhao Wujiu, fundamentally content — he wants nothing from the sect except to keep working wood and practicing in the spring's half-circle. He is the protagonist's unlikely ally in Act 2: the practical man who, when the politics gets thick, asks the question that deflates it ("Will it still need a door?").
**Tong Sijing (童思敬)** — fourteen, early Qi Induction, the sect's youngest disciple and, by Wu Changqing's quiet assessment, its most unusual. Tong Sijing was found by the last recruitment tour at age eleven in a charcoal-burner's hut in the southern foothills, the daughter of a charcoal-burner and a deaf-mute mother who had walked out of the mountains years before and would not say from where. She did not speak Cangli dialect; she spoke a mountain dialect no one in the sect recognized, and she has, in three years, learned Cangli dialect with an accent the other outer disciples tease her about (gently; she is small and watchful and they are mostly kind). She has, however, an inexplicable talent for the founder's water-phase primary route — she picked it up in two weeks where most outer disciples take three months — and a perception of qi-residue that Wu Changqing has, in private, called "Core Formation-clear at Qi Induction," a thing he has never seen in fifty years of cultivation and does not know how to explain.
**Dawn (寅時, roughly 4-6 a.m.):** silent practice at the Green Mirror Spring's half-circle. The protagonist has a reserved seat (Wu Changqing's order; Yan Boyan, who had the seat, was moved one stone to the left). Practice runs 90 minutes. The protagonist practices the sect's water-phase primary route (different from Old Chen's wood-phase route; the protagonist is learning it for the first time) alongside the other dawn-rotation cultivators.
**Early morning (卯時, 6-8 a.m.):** communal breakfast in the Pill Hall — millet porridge with pickled vegetables, with a small bowl of spirit-herb broth twice a week. After breakfast, the protagonist's outer-disciple duties: three mornings a week, herb-garden weeding with Wang Meili (this is how the protagonist and Meili rebuild their cousin-friendship); two mornings, scripture-hall maintenance with Old Liu (sweeping, sanding the warded door, copying damaged manuals); one morning, spirit-vein tap inspection with Peng Li (the protagonist's Foundation Establishment perception is useful here — Peng Li asks, in the protagonist's second week, whether the vein "feels right" at depth, a question Peng Li has wanted to ask a Foundation Establishment cultivator for years).
**Mid-morning to midday (辰時-午時, 8 a.m.-12 p.m.):** sect missions or free practice. Missions are posted on the Merit Hall board; the protagonist, as Foundation Establishment, is offered higher-tier missions (beast suppression in the foothills, escort to Qinghe Market Town, investigation of qi-anomalies in the tenant villages) that pay more spirit stones than the outer-disciple standard. The protagonist may take these or decline; declining is allowed but noted by Su Wenli, who tracks the Merit Hall's books.
**Midday meal:** rice with whatever the kitchen has, eaten in the Pill Hall or, on warm days, on the steps of the Founder's Hall.
**Afternoon (未時-申時, 1-5 p.m.):** free practice, private study, or — by Wu Changqing's invitation, twice a week — private instruction in the sect's higher techniques. These sessions are the protagonist's actual sect education. Wu Changqing teaches the water-phase integration theory (the theory he could not himself complete) and watches the protagonist practice it with the focused, painful hope of a man watching his own unfinished work being finished by another hand.
**Evening (酉時, 5-7 p.m.):** evening meal, then the outer-disciple scripture lecture in the scripture hall. Old Liu teaches the foundational manual; the protagonist attends (Wu Changqing's order, again) and sits at the back. The protagonist already knows the material at a level Old Liu cannot teach, but Wu Changqing's instruction is that the protagonist must be seen to learn the sect's tradition in front of the sect's younger disciples. This is political theater; it works. By the protagonist's third month, the outer disciples who were uneasy about a Foundation Establishment cultivator in their dormitory have decided the protagonist is, in some undefinable way, one of them.
**Night (戌時-亥時, 7-11 p.m.):** private practice in the Long Dormitory (the protagonist has a corner cot, separated from the next by a hanging cloth) or, by the second month, in a small practice room Wu Changqing has had cleared in the Founder's Hall. Sleep by 11.
**Social dynamics:** the protagonist, as Foundation Establishment, is socially anomalous — too powerful to be a true outer disciple, too junior (in sect standing) to be an inner disciple. Yan Boyan treats the protagonist with cold courtesy. Zhao Wujiu treats the protagonist as a friend, which the protagonist, after the long solitude of Old Chen's hermitage, finds almost unbearably welcome. Wang Meili treats the protagonist as a cousin, which means she is both warmer and ruder than anyone else. Su Wenli treats the protagonist as a curiosity, asks careful questions, and writes the answers in a small notebook the protagonist does not see. Ma Yuening develops, in the protagonist's first month, a hero-worship that the protagonist must (gently, carefully) deflect. The Cao twins watch the protagonist for two months before deciding, on the basis of an afternoon the protagonist spent helping them repair a warded door, that the protagonist is acceptable. Peng Li offers the protagonist a piece of carved wood — a small horse, badly done — as a house-warming gift the first week, and never mentions it again. Old Liu, on the protagonist's second night, sits on the cot next to the protagonist's and says, "Welcome home, little cultivator. The tea is in the brown pot. Don't drink the green one, it's Old Chen's leftovers from his visit three years ago and it's gone off." The protagonist, who has not slept in a room with nine other people since childhood, sleeps deeply for the first time in years.
**The second act (ages 220-270):** the sect master's first decade. She inherited a sect at the height of its regional power and presided over its consolidation — the three-vein system (the sect's three spirit veins, all metal-phase primary, were tapped in her first decade), the grotto-heaven's first full ecological survey, the establishment of the intelligence hall (her innovation; the sect had no intelligence capability before her).
**The third act (ages 270-present):** the long, slow turn toward Nascent Soul. She attempted Nascent Soul at age three hundred and ten (failed; the integration collapsed at the spirit-severance phase; her Core Formation was destabilized for a decade and she did not attempt again for thirty years). She attempted again at age three hundred and thirty (failed; this time the tribulation was harder than her Core Formation tribulation and she barely survived). She is now, at three hundred and forty, preparing a third attempt. The third attempt requires resources — spirit stones, grotto-heaven time, concentrated qi — beyond what the sect's three current veins produce. The Cangli Riverlands' latent vein (the deep extension of the Green Mirror Vein, §2.4) is, for her, the resource that funds the third attempt. This is the personal stake beneath the political: a woman who has failed twice and will not survive a third failure, gambling on one more attempt.

**The thirty-six hexagrams of stable, formed states** (Qian 乾, Kun 坤, Tai 泰, Pi 否, et al.) — these name conditions of cosmic equilibrium or its absence. The sixteen-year-old mortal protagonist has not lived long enough to occupy a stable equilibrium; Wang Lun, receiving one of these, would read it as a hexagram about the world's larger motion rather than the protagonist's, and would redirect the reading toward the smaller, more personal hexagram nested inside it (the nuclear hexagram, the hu gua 互卦). The redirection is a real divinatory technique; Wang Lun knows it; the simulation implements it.
**The twelve hexagrams of advanced cultivation states** (Guai 夬, Gou 姤, Bo 剝, Fu 復 — the twelve消息 hexagrams that describe the annual cycle of qi rising and falling) — these name realm-level transitions the protagonist has not yet approached. Wang Lun, receiving one, would read it as a forecast of the protagonist's long cultivation arc rather than a present-state description, and would (correctly) treat it as beyond his ability to interpret fully. He would say, in this case, "I cannot read this hexagram for you. It is too large. Go to Old Chen; he will know what to do with it." This is the only player-state category in which Wang Lun explicitly defers to Old Chen by name in the casting itself, rather than in the direction-giving after.
**The sixteen hexagrams of extreme emotional states** (Dui 兌 in its extreme forms, Zhen 震 in its extreme forms, et al.) — these name conditions of overwhelming joy, rage, terror, or grief that the protagonist could, in principle, occupy at session ~5 (a protagonist who has just learned of Wang Lun's impending pneumonia, a protagonist consumed by rage at Wang Zongde, a protagonist in the grip of unmourned Wang Xiaodi grief so acute it has produced a qi-deviation onset). These are reachable in principle but require specific player choices that the first five sessions make available only at the extremes. Wang Lun, receiving one, would read it carefully and would name what he sees — the protagonist's extremity — and would point to Old Chen with greater urgency than the eight common hexagrams warrant. The protagonist's journal entry, in these cases, is shorter (the protagonist is in no state to write at length) and more fragmentary.
**Reviewer A (the genre traditionalist):** "The Cangwu Sect is too sympathetic and the Azure Sword Sect is too nuanced. The genre wants clearly evil great sects and clearly virtuous small sects. You have given Wu Changqing a notebook and Leng Wushuang a dead husband and a third attempt, and now neither side is a villain. The player will not know whom to root for."
**Response:** The genre's clearly-evil-great-sect convention is the power-fantasy version of xianxia, in which the protagonist's rise is validated by the obvious villainy of those above them. This game's thesis (doc 26 §0, doc 05 §4.3) is that cultivation is becoming less limited, not becoming more powerful than the villains. The political engine of Act 2 requires that the Azure Sword Sect's expansion be lawful (if ruthless) and the Cangwu Sect's resistance be understandable (if doomed), because the protagonist's choice — defend the small sect, mediate, or step aside — is only meaningful if both sides have a real case. Leng Wushuang is not a villain; she is a woman who has failed twice and is gambling on a third attempt, and the resources for the attempt require an expansion the small sect cannot survive. Wu Changqing is not a hero; he is a man who broke himself at twenty-one and has spent thirty years presiding over the slow decline of the sect he inherited, and his grief has curdled into a bitterness he writes about in a notebook no one reads. The player roots for the protagonist, who must navigate between them. This is the doctrine's "confront the central tension directly" (AGENTS.md Part 3): the tension is real, the sides are real, and the protagonist's choice is the engine.
**Reviewer B (the systems designer):** "Nine named disciples at the Cangwu Sect is too many for the simulation to track with the depth you specify. Each disciple has an arc, a faction, a relationship to the protagonist, and a death-or-transformation trajectory. Across Act 2 (sessions 120-500), the simulation must track all nine plus the protagonist plus the Azure Sword Sect's named characters plus the companions. This is a cast of thirty. The simulation will choke."
**Response:** The simulation does not track all nine at full depth at all times. Each disciple has a baseline state (realm, faction, relationship vector) updated per solar term, and a foreground-depth that activates only when the protagonist is in the disciple's presence or when the disciple is the protagonist's current mission-relevant NPC. The nine named disciples are the sect's load-bearing NPCs; the other twenty-one (the unnamed outer and inner disciples) are simulated as a cohort with aggregate state. The cast of thirty is the cast of named, foreground-depth characters across the entire Act 2-3 arc, not the cast of simultaneously-foreground characters in any single session. The precedent is Crusader Kings' character-density model (hundreds of named characters, of which a dozen are foreground at any time) and Baldur's Gate 3's companion model (six companions, of which three are in the party at any time). This is shippable; the depth is calibrated to the foreground/background distinction, not to uniform simulation.
**Reviewer C (the production lead):** "Eight hexagrams with full judgment text, direction-giving, journal entry, and Old Chen response, plus a template for the other fifty-six. This is a writing load you have not budgeted."
**Response:** The eight specified entries are written (this document). The fifty-six template outputs are not fully written; they are produced by the template (§3.10) from the eight calibration points, with the writing team filling in the template's gaps for the fifty-six in a single sprint after the eight are validated. The total writing load is eight full entries (done) plus fifty-six template interpolations (a sprint). The Old Chen responses (§3.11) are eight additional lines of dialogue, written (this document). The journal entries' persistence across the 1500-session arc (the design intent that the same journal text reads differently at session 5 and session 450) is a rendering and UX concern, not a writing concern — the text does not change; the player's reading context does. This is shippable in the Act 1 vertical slice (the eight hexagrams are Scene-1 content) with the fifty-six template interpolations deferred to the Act 2 expansion.
