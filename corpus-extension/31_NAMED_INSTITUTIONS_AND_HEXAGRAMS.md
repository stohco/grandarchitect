## 0. What this document is and why it exists

The five-critic audit (doc 24 §0) found that the Cangwu Sect (蒼梧派) and the Azure Sword Sect (碧劍宗) were named in doc 24 §4.3 but not detailed, and that the 64 hexagrams referenced in Scene 1 (doc 06) were named as a class but not specified. The narrative spine (doc 26) hangs the entire political engine of Act 2 — the Cangli spirit vein dispute, the protagonist's first sect, the romance, the antagonist's first appearance — on these three underspecified objects. This document specifies them. No fork is deferred; every named disciple, every faction, every hexagram is committed.

The protagonist joins the Cangwu Sect at session ~120 (doc 26 §4.2) as a Foundation Establishment cultivator. The sect is small, poor, and politically trapped; the protagonist's arrival is, for the sect, an event. The Azure Sword Sect is the trap. The hexagrams are the language Wang Lun uses to name what the protagonist is becoming, six years before any of this is visible.

No pinyin tone marks appear in this document. Hanzi appear in parentheses on first mention.

## Part 1 — The Cangwu Sect (蒼梧派)

### 1.1 The compound and its valley

The Cangwu Mountains (蒼梧山脈, doc 24 §4.2) are a long granitic arc running roughly southwest to northeast through southern Qingzhou Prefecture. Wang Family Bend sits on the plain below their southern foothills; the Cangwu Sect sits in their central spine, one hundred li west of the bend, in a hanging valley called **Cangwu Valley** (蒼梧谷) at an elevation of roughly 1,400 meters. The valley is roughly two li long and half a li wide, oriented east-west, walled on three sides by granite ridges and open on the east to a steep descent into the foothills. A single paved mule-track — the sect's only road — switchbacks up the eastern wall and through the **Stone Gate** (石門), a natural cleft between two house-sized boulders that is the sect's formal threshold and its only easy access. Above the Stone Gate, painted in fading cinnabar, is the sect's seal: a stylized wutong tree (梧桐) under three short strokes representing the three peaks of the valley's western wall.

Through the valley's center runs **Stone Gate Creek** (石門溪), a fast, cold stream that drops out of a spring on the western wall, crosses the valley floor in a series of small cascades, and exits east through a crack beneath the Stone Gate itself. The creek is the sect's water source, its laundry, and (in summer, when the children are let out of the scripture hall) its swimming hole. Its qi is faintly water-phase — cool, dense, downward-tending — and the sect's outer disciples learn to perceive qi, in their first months, by attending to the creek's surface.

On the valley's north wall, three hundred meters above the creek, a granite shelf juts out: **Holding Sun Cliff** (抱陽崖), so named because it faces due south and catches the full arc of the winter sun. The cliff is where the sect's scripture hall, the sect master's house, and the inner disciples' dormitories are built. A wooden stair of 211 steps climbs from the valley floor to the cliff; the stair is steep and old, and the third step from the top has cracked along its grain, so every disciple in the sect has learned to skip it without thinking. At the top of the stair sits the **Founder's Hall** (祖師堂), a single-room timber building with a tile roof, three ancestor tablets on the altar, and — behind the altar, in a sandalwood box — the founder's token: a thumb-sized jade bead, faintly green, qi-bound to the spirit vein. The bead is the sect master's authority; without it, the elder council can convene but cannot ratify.

The compound's other buildings: the **Merit Hall** (功德堂), where tasks are posted on a board and qi-stone stipends are counted out; the **Scripture Pavilion** (藏經閣), a two-story stone building with a warded door and a single Foundation-locked inner chamber; the **Pill Hall** (丹房), really a kitchen-and-workshop with three small furnaces, two of which work; the **Punishment Shed** (刑房), unused in seven years and currently used to store kindling; and the outer disciples' **Long Dormitory** (長舍), a single building of packed earth and timber with forty cots in two rows. The Long Dormitory is overcapacity at thirty (the sect's inner disciples, twelve of them, sleep in smaller rooms on the cliff); the surplus cots hold stored grain and the corpse of a Foundation Establishment manual so damaged the Scripture Pavilion will not shelve it.

Behind the compound, on the western wall, the spirit vein breaks the surface: a **spring** (青鏡泉, the Green Mirror Spring) in a small cave behind a curtain of moss, where the qi is dense enough to taste — cool, faintly sweet, water-phase dominant with a wood-phase undertone that smells like wet bark. The spring feeds the creek. The sect's main cultivation practice happens in a half-circle of flat stones around the spring's mouth, where outer disciples sit in pre-dawn rotation.

### 1.2 The sect master: Wu Changqing (吳長清)

Wu Changqing is fifty-two years old and looks sixty. He is tall, thin, slightly stooped, with a sparse beard he allows to grow because his wife (dead nine years, in the same fever season that took two of the outer disciples) once said she liked it. He wears the dark blue robe of his office most days, patched at both elbows, and a pair of straw sandals he replaces himself every three months. His dantian, perceived by a Qi Condensation cultivator, is a low, steady warmth — the warmth of a banked fire, not a burning one. His meridians, perceived at the same resolution, carry a faint wrongness in the central conduit: a scar, a kink, the residue of the failed Foundation Establishment integration thirty-one years ago.

He attempted Foundation Establishment at twenty-one, on the advice and with the support of his own teacher (the previous sect master, Daoist Cangwu, who died at seventy-eight of pure old age — the only peaceful death in the sect's recorded line). The integration began well and collapsed at the seventh meridian — the heart-conduit — when Wu Changqing's wood-phase dominant qi met an unprocessed grief (his elder brother had died in a hunting accident six weeks earlier; Wu Changqing had not mourned him; he had gone straight back to practice). The collapse nearly killed him. He survived by aborting the integration at the cost of permanent meridian damage: the heart-conduit narrowed, and the Qi Condensation reservoir has been leaky ever since. He cannot attempt Foundation Establishment again. The narrow conduit would not survive a second integration.

His grief is twofold. The first grief is the brother — unmourned, then turned to grief for his own collapse, then turned to grief for the brother again. The second grief is the sect. He inherited a sect of forty-two disciples and a vein producing a third again its current output; he has presided over the decline of both. The vein is thinning (the Azure Sword Sect's deep-tap upstream draws, he believes without proof, more than the tribute records show); the disciple cohort is shrinking (the last recruitment tour, two years ago, found only three children with spiritual roots in nine villages). He has not said this aloud to anyone. He writes it, in private, in a small notebook he keeps in the founder's hall, behind the altar, in a box that once held a different jade bead.

His ambition is also twofold, and the two strands knot against each other. The first ambition is for the sect: to leave it stronger than he found it — which means, concretely, to find a successor who can break through to Foundation Establishment, which he cannot, and to break or transform the vassalage to the Azure Sword Sect, which he cannot do alone. The second ambition is for himself: to die whole. He has watched Old Chen (he knows Old Chen; they correspond, twice a year, by letter carried through Qinghe Market Town) reach the same Qi Condensation peak and grow old without bitterness. He cannot. He is bitter. He does not know if this is a flaw in him or a flaw in his circumstance, and he writes about this in the notebook too, in passages that have grown longer over the years.

When the protagonist arrives — a Foundation Establishment cultivator, the first to enter the sect in Wu Changqing's lifetime, recommended by Old Chen — Wu Changqing sees, immediately, both his ambitions' resolution. A Foundation Establishment successor. A cultivator strong enough to make the Azure Sword Sect negotiate. He receives the protagonist with formality and a small, private, desperate hope he does his best to hide.

### 1.3 The thirty disciples

The sect has thirty disciples on the rolls at the protagonist's arrival. Twelve are inner disciples (Qi Condensation, mid-to-peak); eighteen are outer (Qi Induction to early Qi Condensation). The eight specified below are the ones whose presence the simulation must track from session ~120 onward, because each will, at some point in Acts 2 or 3, do something the protagonist cannot ignore.

**Wang Meili (王美麗)** — the protagonist's cousin (father's brother's daughter), twenty-two at the protagonist's arrival, early Qi Condensation, healer-track. She entered the sect at fourteen against her father's wishes (her father, the protagonist's uncle Wang Shouye, distrusts Old Chen and by extension all retired cultivators; he sent Meili to the Cangwu Sect because it was a sect, which is to say an institution, which he understands, rather than a hermit, which he does not). She is small, watchful, precise, with calloused hands from the herb garden and a quiet steadiness the sect's other outer disciples find either reassuring or unnerving depending on their own temperaments. She has, by the time the protagonist arrives, been the sect's effective healer for two years — the previous healer, an old inner disciple named Lin Shu, died of a stroke the previous winter and Wu Changqing has not replaced her. Meili's practice is wood-phase (good for healing, good for growth) with a water-phase secondary. She is the protagonist's first ally in the sect and, eventually (doc 26 §9.1), the lineage bridge.

**Yan Boyan (嚴伯言)** — twenty-eight, late Qi Condensation, the protagonist's first rival in the sect. Yan is the son of the headman of Cangwu Sect's largest tenant village, sent to the sect at eight on a sect-recruitment tour. He is broad-shouldered, handsome in a hard-jawed way, meticulous about his appearance, and ambitious in a way that has curdled over twenty years of being the most talented disciple in a sect too small to reward him. He attempted Foundation Establishment at twenty-five and failed — not as catastrophically as Wu Changqing (his meridians are intact) but badly enough that he has not tried again. He believes (without proof) that Wu Changqing has held back a key technique from him. He believes (with some proof) that the Azure Sword Sect would treat him better. He is the leader of what the inner disciples quietly call the **Northern Faction** — those who favor accepting Azure Sword Sect patronage. When the protagonist arrives — a Foundation Establishment cultivator who will, by sect custom, be ranked above Yan despite having done nothing for the sect — Yan's resentment finds a new object. The rivalry is professional at first (spirit-stone allocations, mission assignments, scripture-hall access), then personal, then (by Act 3, depending on player choices) factional.

**Zhao Wujiu (趙五九)** — twenty-four, mid Qi Condensation, the protagonist's first friend in the sect. "Wujiu" is a childhood name (Fifth-Nine, from his birth order in his father's lineage and his mother's) that he never replaced with a courtesy name because, he says, he likes being numbered. He is tall, soft-voiced, ugly in a kind way (a long nose, a crooked smile), and the son of a herdboy from a tenant village. He entered the sect at twelve (a late recruit — the sect found him when he was helping drive cattle past the Stone Gate and a senior disciple noticed he could perceive the creek's qi without instruction). His practice is earth-phase, slow and steady, and his cultivation has been the slowest in his cohort — he is twenty-four and only mid Qi Condensation, where Yan Boyan is twenty-eight and late Qi Condensation. Zhao Wujiu does not mind. He likes the sect. He likes the routine. He likes Wu Changqing. He will become the protagonist's first friend in the sect because he is the only inner disciple who, on the protagonist's first day, talks to the protagonist without calculating.

**Su Wenli (蘇文理)** — thirty-one, Qi Condensation peak, the betrayer. Su is the son of a county-school teacher from Cangli County Seat who paid the sect a silver fee to take his son as an outer disciple at fifteen (a practice the sect accepts when the treasury is low, which is most years). Su is intelligent, well-read, articulate, and possessed of a self-regard that he has learned to disguise as humility. He is the sect's effective Scripture Hall steward (the actual hall head, an elderly inner disciple named Old Liu, is in early-stage cognitive decline and lets Su run the hall). Su is also, as the protagonist will discover over Act 2, in quiet correspondence with Elder Rong Zhaoyuan of the Azure Sword Sect (the Merit Hall head and leader of the Azure Sword expansionists, §2.5). The correspondence began four years ago, after the Azure Sword Sect's last tribute-collection visit, when Rong Zhaoyuan complimented Su on a memo he had written about the Cangwu Sect's vein output. Su does not think of himself as a traitor. He thinks of himself as a realist. The Cangwu Sect is dying; the Azure Sword Sect is the future; the protagonist's arrival is a complication in an arrangement he has spent four years building. He will betray the sect in Act 2 by leaking the protagonist's Foundation Establishment status and the vein's deep-tap location to Rong Zhaoyuan — information that accelerates the Azure Sword Sect's formal claim at session ~300.

**Old Liu (劉師兄, Liu Shixiong)** — forty-seven, mid Qi Condensation, the sect's Scripture Hall head in name and the outer disciples' de facto father in practice. Old Liu entered the sect at seven (the youngest recruit in sect history) and has not left the valley in thirty-eight years. He is short, round, perpetually amused, and unable to read anything more complex than the sect's basic manuals — which is why he let Su Wenli take over the Scripture Hall's administrative work. He teaches the outer disciples' morning scripture class himself (from memory; he has the foundational manual memorized in its entirety, with all Wu Changqing's annotations) and he is the one who, when an outer disciple is crying in the dormitory at two in the morning, gets up and makes them tea. He is, in his way, the sect's actual heart. He will not betray the sect, will not rise in it, and will die (in Act 3, of a stroke, at sixty-one, in the Scripture Hall with a cup of tea in his hand) having never perceived his own anchor.

**Ma Yuening (馬月寧)** — nineteen, early Qi Condensation (broke through three months before the protagonist's arrival; the breakthrough was the sect's social event of the year), the youngest inner disciple. She is from a fishing family on the Eastern Sea coast — recruited on the same tour that produced Yan Boyan — and she is, in temperament, the opposite of Yan: open, excitable, generous with her opinions, prone to crushes (on Yan at sixteen, on Su at eighteen, on Zhao Wujiu at nineteen — the last of which Zhao has not noticed). Her practice is water-phase (matching the vein), and she is the most talented pure cultivator the sect has produced in two generations. Wu Changqing, privately, believes she might be the one — the disciple who reaches Foundation Establishment legitimately. He has not told her this; he is afraid of putting the same pressure on her that broke him.

**The Cao Twins — Cao Shan (曹山) and Cao Hai (曹海)** — both thirty, late Qi Condensation, sons of a deceased inner disciple (Cao Ping, who died in a beast hunt twelve years ago) and raised in the sect. They are not identical — Cao Shan is the older by half an hour, taller, slower, kinder; Cao Hai is shorter, quicker, sharper — but they have spent thirty years finishing each other's sentences and the sect has stopped trying to tell them apart except by height. They are Yan Boyan's wingmen in the Northern Faction, more out of lifelong habit than conviction. They will, if the protagonist befriends Yan, follow Yan; if the protagonist befriends them, they will (reluctantly, then with relief) follow the protagonist. They are the sect's swing vote in any inner-disciple dispute.

**Peng Li (彭力)** — thirty-five, mid Qi Condensation, the sect's woodworker and the dormitory's eldest occupant. He is the only disciple who entered the sect as an adult (he was a carpenter in Qinghe Market Town; he perceived qi at twenty-eight after a long illness and walked to the Stone Gate on foot; Wu Changqing admitted him on the spot). He is quiet, slow-speaking, large-handed, and the only disciple in the sect who can repair a warded door or rebuild a qi-channel in the spirit-vein tap. He is indispensable and knows it. He is also, like Zhao Wujiu, fundamentally content — he wants nothing from the sect except to keep working wood and practicing in the spring's half-circle. He is the protagonist's unlikely ally in Act 2: the practical man who, when the politics gets thick, asks the question that deflates it ("Will it still need a door?").

**Tong Sijing (童思敬)** — fourteen, early Qi Induction, the sect's youngest disciple and, by Wu Changqing's quiet assessment, its most unusual. Tong Sijing was found by the last recruitment tour at age eleven in a charcoal-burner's hut in the southern foothills, the daughter of a charcoal-burner and a deaf-mute mother who had walked out of the mountains years before and would not say from where. She did not speak Cangli dialect; she spoke a mountain dialect no one in the sect recognized, and she has, in three years, learned Cangli dialect with an accent the other outer disciples tease her about (gently; she is small and watchful and they are mostly kind). She has, however, an inexplicable talent for the founder's water-phase primary route — she picked it up in two weeks where most outer disciples take three months — and a perception of qi-residue that Wu Changqing has, in private, called "Core Formation-clear at Qi Induction," a thing he has never seen in fifty years of cultivation and does not know how to explain.

Tong Sijing does not know how to explain it either. She says, when asked, that the qi "has always been there, the same way the creek has always been there," and that she does not understand why this is remarkable. Wu Changqing has not told anyone else about her residue-perception; he is afraid of what it might mean and afraid of what the Azure Sword Sect would do if they knew. He has written, in his notebook, in the small careful hand he uses for the entries that frighten him: "If the vein has produced a perception-talent, the vein is deeper than we know." Tong Sijing, for her part, is too young to have an arc; she has, instead, a trajectory — a line the simulation must track from her arrival to her late teens, when her talent will either flower into something the sect cannot hide or be quietly suppressed by a sect master who knows what the Azure Sword Sect does to perception-talents it cannot recruit. The protagonist, on arrival, notices her in the first week (she is the only disciple younger than the protagonist, and the only one who watches the protagonist practice without calculation). The protagonist's relationship with Tong Sijing, across Act 2, is the relationship of a senior cultivator who sees a junior talent and must decide what to do about it — teach her, hide her, or, hardest, let her find her own way.

### 1.4 The spirit vein: the Green Mirror Vein (青鏡脈)

The sect's spirit vein is the **Green Mirror Vein** (青鏡脈), a yin-dominant, water-phase-primary vein with a wood-phase secondary, running northeast-southwest through the granite of the central Cangwu range. Its surface trace is the Green Mirror Spring (§1.1); its main current lies roughly 240 meters below the valley floor, flowing slowly northeast at an estimated rate of (the sect's last measurement, fifteen years ago) enough to sustain thirty-five to forty Qi Condensation cultivators at full practice. The vein's history: it was discovered 240 years ago by a wandering Qi Condensation cultivator named Daoist Cangwu (the founder's courtesy name; his personal name is not recorded), who settled the valley, built the Founder's Hall, and slowly attracted the first disciples. The sect was formally constituted 180 years ago (doc 24 §4.4), at which point the vein's output was nearly double its current level. The decline is real (Wu Changqing's notebook records it; the Azure Sword Sect's tribute-collectors know it); the cause is contested. Wu Changqing believes the Azure Sword Sect's deep-tap upstream — at the Jade Edge Grotto (§2.3) — is drawing more than the tribute records state. The Azure Sword Sect maintains the decline is geological, the vein aging naturally. The vein's phase-affinity shapes the sect's entire practice tradition: the founder's manual is a water-phase primary circulation with wood-phase secondary integration, and every disciple the sect has ever produced has practiced it. This is why the sect's cultivators tend toward healer and diviner paths and away from combat — water and wood are gentle phases. It is also why the Azure Sword Sect (metal-phase) considers the Cangwu Sect's tradition soft and the Cangwu Sect's territory, by extension, an underdeveloped resource.

### 1.5 The relationship to the Azure Sword Sect: vassalage

The Cangwu Sect is, in everything but name, a vassal of the Azure Sword Sect. The arrangement is not formalized in a treaty (the Azure Sword Sect does not treaty with Qi Condensation sects); it is a generational fact sustained by three things. First, **tribute**: the Cangwu Sect sends, every autumn, a chest of six low-grade spirit stones and four bundles of wood-phase spirit herbs (the sect's only export-grade crop) to the Azure Sword Sect, delivered by outer disciple escort to the foothills and there handed over to an Azure Sword Sect outer disciple who comes down to meet them. The tribute is roughly a quarter of the sect's annual surplus; Wu Changqing pays it because not paying it would invite a visit. Second, **protection**: in theory, the Azure Sword Sect defends the Cangwu Sect from external threats. In practice, the last Azure Sword Sect intervention on the Cangwu Sect's behalf was twelve years ago, when a Foundation Establishment beast (a jade-scaled python from the southern foothills) threatened the tenant villages and Wu Changqing, whose own combat ability is limited, requested help. The Azure Sword Sect sent a single Foundation Establishment elder, who killed the python in an afternoon and stayed the night in the Founder's Hall and complimented the tea. Third, **non-competition**: the Cangwu Sect does not recruit in villages the Azure Sword Sect claims; the Azure Sword Sect does not, in principle, recruit in the Cangwu Sect's territory. In practice, the Azure Sword Sect's definition of "its territory" extends to the entire central Cangwu range, and the Cangwu Sect's effective recruitment zone is six villages within thirty li of the Stone Gate.

The relationship is resented. Every disciple in the sect knows the tribute is excessive; every inner disciple knows the protection is theoretical; Yan Boyan and Su Wenli know, in addition, that the arrangement is structurally unchangeable from below. Wu Changqing's resentment is the most private and the most pointed: his teacher (the previous sect master) was friends, in his youth, with the Azure Sword Sect's previous sect master (the Zhou Wugui-killer, doc 24 §4.4) — they had hunted together once, in the foothills, as young cultivators. That friendship was the reason the tribute, in the previous sect master's time, was three stones and two bundles. After the previous Azure Sword sect master died and Leng Wushuang succeeded him, the tribute was "reassessed" upward. Wu Changqing has never forgiven this. He has also never mentioned it to anyone except Old Liu, who is the only one who remembers the old amount.

### 1.6 Internal politics: three factions and the succession question

The sect's internal politics are organized, at the protagonist's arrival, into three informal factions. None is named; all are real.

The **Old Mountain faction** is led by Old Liu (in his amiable, distracted way) and includes Peng Li, Ma Yuening, and most of the outer disciples by default. Its position: the sect's tradition is correct, the vassalage is regrettable but endurable, and the path forward is patient cultivation and the hope of a Foundation Establishment successor. The Old Mountain faction is the largest and the quietest; it does not meet as a faction, it does not strategize, it is simply the disposition of the sect's contented majority.

The **Northern faction** (Yan Boyan, Su Wenli, the Cao twins, and two other inner disciples) favors accepting formal Azure Sword Sect patronage. The argument: the Cangwu Sect cannot survive another generation at its current trajectory; the Azure Sword Sect offers resources, recruitment access, and a path for ambitious disciples (Yan) into the larger sect; the sect's independence is, in any case, a fiction. The Northern faction is the most politically active; it meets, in effect, whenever Yan and Su walk together in the valley, which is most evenings. The Northern faction's internal tension: Su Wenli is in correspondence with the Azure Sword Sect (§1.3); Yan Boyan does not know this. Yan thinks the Northern faction's strategy is to negotiate from a position of internal consensus. Su knows the negotiation is, in fact, already happening, and the question is only what terms the Cangwu Sect will accept.

The **Independence faction** is Wu Changqing alone, with the protagonist (on arrival) as its only potential ally. Its position: the vassalage must be broken, the vein must be defended, the Azure Sword Sect is a predator, and the path forward is to find allies outside the central Cangwu range — the Jade Void Holy Land (distant, but historically interested in the Cangli Riverlands), the rogue cultivator network (Old Chen's connections, which the protagonist inherits), or the Wang lineage itself (which is, after the protagonist's Foundation Establishment, technically a cultivation-lineage-in-waiting). The Independence faction is the smallest and the most desperate; Wu Changqing knows he cannot win a confrontation with the Azure Sword Sect and is betting everything on the protagonist.

The succession question: Wu Changqing has no children and no designated heir. The elder council, in the sect's history, has convened twice to discuss succession — once when Wu Changqing was forty (he fought off a quiet movement to designate Yan Boyan as heir-apparent) and once when he was forty-eight (after a chest infection that winter that frightened everyone, including him). The council is, in practice, the inner disciples plus Old Liu, and the question is who will be sect master when Wu Changqing ages out or dies. The candidates, in descending order of likelihood at the protagonist's arrival: Yan Boyan (the senior inner disciple, ambitious, the Northern faction's choice); the protagonist (Foundation Establishment, the only cultivator in the sect qualified by realm, but a newcomer with no faction); Su Wenli (Qi Condensation peak, intelligent, but scarier to the Old Mountain faction than Yan); Ma Yuening (talented, young, but three years a Qi Condensation cultivator and not yet ready). Wu Changqing's private preference, which he has not stated and will not state until forced: the protagonist, with Ma Yuening as a future successor after Foundation Establishment. This preference is the engine of the sect's Act 2 internal tension.

### 1.7 The protagonist's daily life as an outer disciple

The protagonist, despite being Foundation Establishment, enters the sect as an outer disciple — a deliberate choice by Wu Changqing, endorsed by Old Chen's letter, on the grounds that the protagonist has no sect training and must learn the sect's tradition from the bottom. The arrangement is unusual and the sect knows it; the outer disciples, on the protagonist's first week, watch the protagonist carry water and weed the herb garden with a mixture of awe and unease. The protagonist's daily schedule, from session ~120 onward:

**Dawn (寅時, roughly 4-6 a.m.):** silent practice at the Green Mirror Spring's half-circle. The protagonist has a reserved seat (Wu Changqing's order; Yan Boyan, who had the seat, was moved one stone to the left). Practice runs 90 minutes. The protagonist practices the sect's water-phase primary route (different from Old Chen's wood-phase route; the protagonist is learning it for the first time) alongside the other dawn-rotation cultivators.

**Early morning (卯時, 6-8 a.m.):** communal breakfast in the Pill Hall — millet porridge with pickled vegetables, with a small bowl of spirit-herb broth twice a week. After breakfast, the protagonist's outer-disciple duties: three mornings a week, herb-garden weeding with Wang Meili (this is how the protagonist and Meili rebuild their cousin-friendship); two mornings, scripture-hall maintenance with Old Liu (sweeping, sanding the warded door, copying damaged manuals); one morning, spirit-vein tap inspection with Peng Li (the protagonist's Foundation Establishment perception is useful here — Peng Li asks, in the protagonist's second week, whether the vein "feels right" at depth, a question Peng Li has wanted to ask a Foundation Establishment cultivator for years).

**Mid-morning to midday (辰時-午時, 8 a.m.-12 p.m.):** sect missions or free practice. Missions are posted on the Merit Hall board; the protagonist, as Foundation Establishment, is offered higher-tier missions (beast suppression in the foothills, escort to Qinghe Market Town, investigation of qi-anomalies in the tenant villages) that pay more spirit stones than the outer-disciple standard. The protagonist may take these or decline; declining is allowed but noted by Su Wenli, who tracks the Merit Hall's books.

**Midday meal:** rice with whatever the kitchen has, eaten in the Pill Hall or, on warm days, on the steps of the Founder's Hall.

**Afternoon (未時-申時, 1-5 p.m.):** free practice, private study, or — by Wu Changqing's invitation, twice a week — private instruction in the sect's higher techniques. These sessions are the protagonist's actual sect education. Wu Changqing teaches the water-phase integration theory (the theory he could not himself complete) and watches the protagonist practice it with the focused, painful hope of a man watching his own unfinished work being finished by another hand.

**Evening (酉時, 5-7 p.m.):** evening meal, then the outer-disciple scripture lecture in the scripture hall. Old Liu teaches the foundational manual; the protagonist attends (Wu Changqing's order, again) and sits at the back. The protagonist already knows the material at a level Old Liu cannot teach, but Wu Changqing's instruction is that the protagonist must be seen to learn the sect's tradition in front of the sect's younger disciples. This is political theater; it works. By the protagonist's third month, the outer disciples who were uneasy about a Foundation Establishment cultivator in their dormitory have decided the protagonist is, in some undefinable way, one of them.

**Night (戌時-亥時, 7-11 p.m.):** private practice in the Long Dormitory (the protagonist has a corner cot, separated from the next by a hanging cloth) or, by the second month, in a small practice room Wu Changqing has had cleared in the Founder's Hall. Sleep by 11.

**Social dynamics:** the protagonist, as Foundation Establishment, is socially anomalous — too powerful to be a true outer disciple, too junior (in sect standing) to be an inner disciple. Yan Boyan treats the protagonist with cold courtesy. Zhao Wujiu treats the protagonist as a friend, which the protagonist, after the long solitude of Old Chen's hermitage, finds almost unbearably welcome. Wang Meili treats the protagonist as a cousin, which means she is both warmer and ruder than anyone else. Su Wenli treats the protagonist as a curiosity, asks careful questions, and writes the answers in a small notebook the protagonist does not see. Ma Yuening develops, in the protagonist's first month, a hero-worship that the protagonist must (gently, carefully) deflect. The Cao twins watch the protagonist for two months before deciding, on the basis of an afternoon the protagonist spent helping them repair a warded door, that the protagonist is acceptable. Peng Li offers the protagonist a piece of carved wood — a small horse, badly done — as a house-warming gift the first week, and never mentions it again. Old Liu, on the protagonist's second night, sits on the cot next to the protagonist's and says, "Welcome home, little cultivator. The tea is in the brown pot. Don't drink the green one, it's Old Chen's leftovers from his visit three years ago and it's gone off." The protagonist, who has not slept in a room with nine other people since childhood, sleeps deeply for the first time in years.

### 1.8 The founder's manual and the practice tradition

The Cangwu Sect's founder (Daoist Cangwu, §1.4) left a single manual — the **Green Mirror Water-Wood Scripture** (青鏡水木經) — stored in the Scripture Pavilion's Foundation-locked inner chamber. The manual is short: 4,200 characters across three scrolls, hand-copied onto paper now yellowed and brittle, the founder's own brushwork (a small, precise, slightly cramped hand that Wu Changqing has spent thirty years studying and still finds, in places, opaque). The first scroll describes the water-phase primary circulation route (the route the outer disciples practice at the spring's half-circle at dawn). The second scroll describes the wood-phase secondary integration (the route the inner disciples add at mid Qi Condensation). The third scroll describes the Foundation Establishment integration theory — the route Wu Changqing attempted at twenty-one and could not complete.

The third scroll is the sect's central tension. The first two scrolls are sufficient for Qi Condensation; any disciple with patience can practice them safely. The third scroll is the route to Foundation Establishment, and it is the route that broke Wu Changqing. The route's difficulty is not in its qi-circulation (the route is, by all accounts, elegant and well-designed) but in its psychospiritual demand: the water-wood integration requires the cultivator to hold a grief-stable heart-mind at the moment of integration, because water-phase (associated with grief in the five-phase emotion correspondence) and wood-phase (associated with anger) must be balanced against each other under pressure. A cultivator with unprocessed grief cannot balance them. Wu Changqing had unprocessed grief (his brother's death) and the integration collapsed. Old Chen, who studied the same manual at the Cangwu Sect sixty years ago (Old Chen was, briefly, a Cangwu Sect disciple before he left in his dispute with the previous-previous sect master), also failed — for the same reason, in his case the grief of a different kind.

The protagonist arrives at the Cangwu Sect with two advantages Wu Changqing and Old Chen lacked. First, the protagonist has already achieved Foundation Establishment (via Old Chen's teaching, which was not the Cangwu manual but a wood-phase primary route Old Chen developed independently). The protagonist's Foundation Establishment is therefore not at risk; the third scroll is, for the protagonist, a text to study rather than a route to attempt. Second, the protagonist has (per Scene 4 and Scene 5) already processed the grief of Wang Xiaodi — the protagonist's own water-phase heart-mind is stable. This means the protagonist can, in principle, teach the third scroll's route to a Cangwu Sect disciple who has not yet attempted Foundation Establishment. Ma Yuening is the obvious candidate. Wu Changqing's private hope (§1.6) is, in concrete terms, the hope that the protagonist will teach Ma Yuening the third scroll's route and that Ma Yuening will succeed where Wu Changqing failed.

### 1.9 The seasonal rhythm and the ritual year

The Cangwu Sect's year is organized around the twenty-four solar terms (二十四節氣, doc 00 §4), with four days of particular sect-weight. **The Spring Founding Day** (立春後第三日, the third day after the Beginning of Spring) commemorates the founder's arrival in the valley; the sect observes it with a recitation of the manual's first scroll in the Founder's Hall and a small feast (rice with pickled fish, a luxury the sect cannot really afford). **The Summer Tribute Day** (夏至前五日, five days before the Summer Solstice) is when the autumn tribute is prepared: the spirit stones are counted out, the herb bundles are tied, and the outer-disciple escort is chosen (the protagonist, by their second year, is the obvious choice; the Azure Sword Sect's receiving disciple is, by then, used to seeing them). **The Autumn Recruitment Tour** (秋分前後, around the Autumn Equinox) sends Wu Changqing and one inner disciple (Yan Boyan, by tradition, though Wu Changqing would prefer Zhao Wujiu) through the six tenant villages and three further-flung villages to test children for spiritual roots. The tour is the sect's lifeblood; a year with no recruits is a year the sect's future dims. **The Winter Solstice Vigil** (冬至守夜) is the sect's only night-cultivation: the entire sect sits in the spring's half-circle from sundown to sunrise, the one night the outer disciples are excused from their dormitory curfew. The vigil is cold, long, and quietly beloved. Old Liu makes tea every hour.

The protagonist's first year in the sect is structured by these four days. The first Recruitment Tour the protagonist attends (autumn of the protagonist's first year, age 23 in mortal time but the sect does not track mortal ages) is the tour where the protagonist, accompanying Wu Changqing, returns to Wang Family Bend for the first time since Foundation Establishment — the first return (doc 26 §5.3, §8, the viral moment). The protagonist, walking into the village with Wu Changqing and Yan Boyan behind them, perceives the qi of the place and weeps. Wu Changqing, perceiving the protagonist's state with Qi Condensation perception (he cannot perceive the grief's content, only its disturbance), says nothing. Yan Boyan, perceiving nothing he can name, says nothing either. The tour, that year, finds two children with spiritual roots in Wang Family Bend's cousin-branch. One of them, a girl of seven named Wang Yanrong (王燕容, distant cousin), will, in Act 3, become the protagonist's second disciple in the "found a lineage" ending. This is the structural reason the Recruitment Tour scene is load-bearing: it is where the lineage's future is seeded, even as the protagonist is grieving the lineage's past.

### 1.10 The Wang lineage as latent cultivation lineage

The protagonist's arrival at the Cangwu Sect as a Foundation Establishment cultivator is, for the Wang lineage (the mortal lineage of Wang Family Bend, doc 04 §2.1), a structural transformation the lineage itself does not yet understand. Per doc 12 §2.1, a mortal lineage becomes a cultivation lineage (修真宗族) when it has produced at least one Nascent Soul cultivator whose anchor persists in bardo. The Wang lineage has not done this. But it has, now, produced a Foundation Establishment cultivator — the protagonist — and a Foundation Establishment cultivator is, per doc 12 §2.5, a candidate for lineage office and a potential future lineage head. The Wang lineage is, in the cultivation world's classification, a **latent cultivation lineage** — a mortal lineage that has produced its first cultivator of significant realm and is, thereby, on the path (across generations, if the cultivator's descendants also cultivate) to cultivation-lineage status.

This status is invisible to the Wang lineage itself. The lineage head, Wang Shouzheng, knows the protagonist has "become a cultivator" in the way villagers know such things — a mixture of pride, fear, and the conviction that the protagonist has left the lineage's world for one the lineage cannot follow. He does not know that the protagonist's Foundation Establishment has, in the cultivation world's ledgers, made the Wang lineage a thing of mild interest to mid-level sect intelligence halls (the kind of interest that produces a single line in a single report, filed and forgotten). He does not know that the protagonist's eventual death (if the protagonist reaches Nascent Soul and persists their anchor) will, nine generations from now, make the Wang lineage's hall a qi-anchor that Core Formation descendants can commune with. He does not know that the protagonist's joining the Cangwu Sect — a Wang-lineage cultivator in a Cangwu-Sect robe — is, in the cultivation world's quiet accounting, the first formal link between the Wang lineage and a sect, and that this link will, across the protagonist's long life, become the spine of the Wang lineage's cultivation-future.

Wu Changqing understands all of this. It is, in part, why he admitted the protagonist as an outer disciple despite the protagonist's Foundation Establishment realm — the protagonist, in the sect's robes and the sect's dormitory, is the protagonist's lineage's first emissary to the cultivation world, and Wu Changqing wants the protagonist to learn the sect's tradition from the bottom not as humiliation but as inheritance. The protagonist, when they eventually leave the Cangwu Sect (in Act 2 or Act 3, depending on player choices), will carry the Cangwu Sect's water-phase manual with them — the founder's manual, copied by the protagonist's own hand, the third scroll now comprehensible to a Foundation Establishment cultivator who has already crossed the route it describes. This copy is, in the cultivation world's accounting, the Wang lineage's first cultivation manual. It will be stored, eventually, in the Wang lineage hall (or in Old Chen's hermitage, which the protagonist inherits at session ~450, doc 26 §12.4). It will be the seed of the Wang lineage's cultivation school (doc 26 §7.1, ending 1). Wu Changqing, giving the protagonist access to the manual in the protagonist's first month, knows all of this and says nothing. He writes, in his notebook, in the careful hand: "The Wang lineage will outlast us. This is well."

## Part 2 — The Azure Sword Sect (碧劍宗)

### 2.1 The compound and the grotto-heaven's anchor

The Azure Sword Sect sits on **Biluo Peak** (碧螺峰, the Green-Spiral Peak), the highest of the Northern Cangwu range, five hundred li north of Wang Family Bend. The peak is a single granite massif rising 2,800 meters above the surrounding highland, with a flat upper third that the sect has terraced into seven cultivated ledges. The compound walls — twenty meters of dark stone, qi-reinforced, with three gates (the South Gate for disciples, the East Gate for supply caravans, the North Gate for the sect master and honored guests) — enclose the upper third entirely. Inside the walls: the **Sword-Ancestor Hall** (劍祖堂, the founder's hall, where the founder's sword is enshrined rather than a jade bead — the Azure Sword Sect's token is a sword, a single-edge straight sword of unknown metal, three feet long, that the founder carried down from the peak's summit the day she broke through to Core Formation); the **Three Sword Peaks** (三劍峰, three sub-peaks within the wall, each held by a peak master); the **Eight Halls** (merit, scripture, pill, punishment, sword-craft, formation, intelligence, and guest — the Azure Sword Sect's bureaucratic tier is more developed than the Cangwu Sect's, befitting a sect of four hundred); the **inner disciples' courtyard city** (a dense grid of two-story buildings housing three hundred inner disciples); the **outer disciples' camp** (a less dense grid on the lowest terrace, housing one hundred outer disciples); and the **Founder's Garden** (a quiet courtyard with a single wutong tree, the sect's emblem, where the founder is said to have sat the night before her Core Formation breakthrough).

Behind the compound, beneath the North Gate, the grotto-heaven's anchor: the **Sword-Washing Waterfall** (洗劍瀑), a sixty-meter fall from a spring on the peak's north face into a deep, cold pool. Behind the waterfall, in the cliff, a jade gate — a single slab of green-veined white jade, two meters tall, one meter wide, carved with the founder's sword-seal. The gate is the grotto-heaven's anchor (doc 19 §2.3). A cultivator with the sect's sword qi-signature may pass through the jade as if through water; a cultivator without it cannot. The gate is the only entrance. Destroying it (an act that would require a Spirit Severance cultivator sustained over hours, or a Mahayana cultivator in moments) would seal the grotto-heaven; the Azure Sword Sect has, in its 180-year history, never had to defend it.

### 2.2 The sect master: Leng Wushuang (冷無霜)

Leng Wushuang is three hundred and forty years old and looks thirty-five. She is tall, narrow-shouldered, with a face that the sect's outer disciples privately call "the cold face" — high cheekbones, a long straight nose, dark eyes that observe without appearing to, and hair (still black, no gray) she wears in a single braid down her back, fastened with a metal hairpin that is also a sword. She wears white. She has worn white every day for seventy years, since her husband Mu Tianqing (穆天青, Foundation Establishment, a sword cultivator of her own sect) died in a tribulation gone wrong; the white is mourning that has long since become identity. Her Core Formation qi, perceived by another Core Formation cultivator, is dense, cold, sharp — metal-phase primary, water-phase secondary, perfectly disciplined. Her dantian, perceived at Foundation Establishment resolution, looks like a small, bright, cold star.

She was not born into the Azure Sword Sect. She was a rogue Core Formation cultivator from the eastern provinces who arrived at Biluo Peak at age ninety-two, seeking the founder's manual on sword-cultivation (the Azure Sword Sect's library, even then, was the best in the central Cangwu range). The previous sect master (the Zhou Wugui-killer) admitted her as a senior guest, then as an elder, then (after thirty years of demonstrated loyalty) as his designated successor. She became sect master at age two hundred and sixty, eighty years ago, when the previous master died of old age. She has held the seat since.

Her history, in three acts. **The first act (ages 90-220):** the rogue cultivator who became an insider. She earned the sect's trust by fighting, by teaching, and by refusing to take a peak master's seat until the council offered it twice. She married Mu Tianqing at age one hundred and fifty (he was her junior by sixty years; the match scandalized the conservative elders and delighted the rest); they were together for one hundred and twenty years.

**The second act (ages 220-270):** the sect master's first decade. She inherited a sect at the height of its regional power and presided over its consolidation — the three-vein system (the sect's three spirit veins, all metal-phase primary, were tapped in her first decade), the grotto-heaven's first full ecological survey, the establishment of the intelligence hall (her innovation; the sect had no intelligence capability before her).

**The third act (ages 270-present):** the long, slow turn toward Nascent Soul. She attempted Nascent Soul at age three hundred and ten (failed; the integration collapsed at the spirit-severance phase; her Core Formation was destabilized for a decade and she did not attempt again for thirty years). She attempted again at age three hundred and thirty (failed; this time the tribulation was harder than her Core Formation tribulation and she barely survived). She is now, at three hundred and forty, preparing a third attempt. The third attempt requires resources — spirit stones, grotto-heaven time, concentrated qi — beyond what the sect's three current veins produce. The Cangli Riverlands' latent vein (the deep extension of the Green Mirror Vein, §2.4) is, for her, the resource that funds the third attempt. This is the personal stake beneath the political: a woman who has failed twice and will not survive a third failure, gambling on one more attempt.

Her personality is disciplined, principled by discipline rather than by conviction, and cold in a way that is not unkindness but efficiency. She does not waste words. She does not waste qi. She does not waste affection. She loved her husband; she has not, since his death, allowed herself the kind of attachment that could distract her from the third attempt. She loves her daughter (Leng Qingxue, born when Leng Wushuang was two hundred and twenty) in the same disciplined way — carefully, with attention, but without the softness that she associates (correctly, she would say) with her own failed breakthroughs. She is aware that her daughter opposes her. She has not, in twelve years, mentioned this to her daughter directly. She has mentioned it, twice, to her own journal, which is kept in the Sword-Ancestor Hall behind the founder's sword and which no one else has read.

Her relationship to the Cangli Riverlands is structural. The Azure Sword Sect's territory, properly understood, is the Northern Cangwu range; the Cangli Riverlands are below the range, on the plain. But the Cangli Riverlands' vein system (the Green Mirror Vein and its deep extension) is geologically continuous with the Northern Cangwu veins — the same granitic arc, the same fault system. Leng Wushuang believes (correctly, per the vein map her intelligence hall has produced) that the Green Mirror Vein's main current, deep below the Cangwu Sect's shallow tap, runs northeast into the Azure Sword Sect's territory at depth. She considers this vein, at depth, legitimately contestable. She considers the Cangwu Sect's shallow tap a historical accident. She has not said this to Wu Changqing. She has said it, in formal terms, to the Azure Sword Sect's elder council, who have agreed.

### 2.3 The grotto-heaven: the Jade Edge Grotto (碧玉緣洞天)

The Jade Edge Grotto is a major grotto-heaven (doc 19 §2.1) of approximately eighty square kilometers — a mountain valley inside the pocket, four kilometers long and two wide, with a central lake (the **Sword-Mirror Lake**, 劍鏡湖, eight kilometers in circumference, cold, clear, metal-phase-saturated) fed by three streams descending from interior peaks (the grotto has its own sky, a pale blue-white false sun that does not move; the light is always mid-morning). The grotto's qi-climate is metal-phase dominant (cool, sharp, faintly astringent — a cultivator breathing it feels their meridians tighten slightly, which is the metal-phase effect on the lung and large-intestine channels) with water-phase secondary (the lake, the streams, the perpetual mist). Time rate is 1:1 with the Mortal Stratum.

The grotto's law context differs from the Mortal Stratum in one respect: the metal-phase generation cycle is intensified, meaning metal-phase cultivation is approximately 30% faster inside than outside, and wood-phase cultivation (metal conquers wood in the five-phase cycle) is approximately 20% slower. This is why the Azure Sword Sect is a metal-phase sword sect — the grotto made it so. The founder was metal-phase already; the grotto's qi-climate, over six generations of cultivators, has shaped the sect's entire practice tradition.

The grotto's ecology (per doc 19 §3) is isolation-evolved. The herb population includes **sword-grass** (劍草, a sedge with blade-shaped leaves that cut unprotected skin; the basis of the sect's metal-phase healing poultices), **blue-flame lotus** (青焰蓮, a lotus that grows in the Sword-Mirror Lake's shallows and whose petals burn with a cold blue qi-flame when bruised; the sect's most valuable pill ingredient), and **iron-root pine** (鐵根松, a pine whose roots are dense enough to be forged like low-grade iron; the sect's sword-craft hall uses iron-root pine wood for practice swords). The beast population includes **silver-furred foxes** (銀狐, mid-Qi Condensation equivalent, the grotto's dominant predator, intelligent, curious, and dangerous in packs), **metal-phase hawks** (鐵羽鷹, late Qi Condensation, territorial, nesting on the interior peaks), and a single **ancient beast**: a jade-scaled serpent (玉鱗蛇, Foundation Establishment equivalent, six meters long, that lives in the lake's deep and is, by sect tradition, left alone — it was here when the founder arrived, and the founder's manual records her decision to coexist with it). The serpent is the grotto's tacit guardian; no Azure Sword Sect cultivator has ever harmed it, and the sect's intelligence hall has, in the last thirty years, observed Foundation-foreign cultivators attempting to enter the grotto (rogue cultivators, mostly, seeking pills or herbs) and being driven off by the serpent before the sect's patrol could respond.

The grotto is the sect's primary cultivation accelerator. Three hundred and seventy of the sect's four hundred disciples cycle through it on six-month rotations; the remaining thirty (the sect master, the three peak masters, the eight hall heads, and the inner disciples currently on sect duty) practice in the compound's main cultivation hall, which is fed by a direct tap from one of the three surface veins. The grotto's rotations are managed by the merit hall; access is the sect master's prerogative and the most contested resource in the sect's internal politics.

### 2.4 The expansionist interest: the Cangli spirit vein dispute

The Azure Sword Sect's expansionist interest, formalized at session ~300 (doc 26 §4.2), is in the deep extension of the Green Mirror Vein. The intelligence hall's vein map (produced over thirty years by Foundation Establishment elders using the deep-perception method described in doc 14 §1.3) shows the vein's main current running northeast at depth, passing beneath the Cangwu Sect's shallow tap and continuing under the central Cangwu range's granitic spine to a point roughly sixty li southwest of Biluo Peak. At that point, the vein's current is dense enough to support a second grotto-heaven — if an anchor can be established and a grotto-heaven opened (an act requiring, per doc 19, a Nascent Soul cultivator's sustained attention over months).

The Azure Sword Sect's formal claim, when it is filed with the Immortal Alliance (doc 12 §6) at session ~300, asserts that the vein's deep extension is part of the Azure Sword Sect's geological territory (the granitic spine is continuous with Biluo Peak's massif) and that the Cangwu Sect's shallow tap is an overdraw that threatens the vein's long-term health. The Cangwu Sect's counter-claim asserts that the vein's surface trace has been the Cangwu Sect's for 180 years, that the deep extension is part of the same vein, and that the Azure Sword Sect's intelligence hall's vein map is, in fact, intelligence reconnaissance for an act of aggression. The Immortal Alliance's adjudication (which will, by Act 3, have not concluded — the Alliance is slow) is the formal political backdrop. The informal political backdrop is the Azure Sword Sect's clear willingness to act on its claim regardless of adjudication: the sect has, by session ~300, sent two "geological survey" teams into the central Cangwu range, both of which the Cangwu Sect's outer disciples have observed and reported. Wu Changqing's notebook, by this point, contains a third column: dates, observed survey-team sizes, and the protagonist's name written in the margin next to a question mark.

The dispute is the political tension of Act 2. The protagonist is caught between Wu Changqing (who wants the protagonist to defend the sect), Leng Qingxue (who wants the protagonist to mediate), and the protagonist's own interest (which is, primarily, in Pei Liang, who is the actual threat, not the Azure Sword Sect). The protagonist's choices in the dispute (which side to support, which intelligence to share, which duels to fight in the Exchange of Cuts with Leng Qingxue) shape the Act 2 political landscape and determine whether the Cangwu Sect survives the dispute intact.

### 2.5 Internal factions: the expansionists and the isolationists

The Azure Sword Sect's four hundred disciples and thirty-eight elders are organized, at the protagonist's arrival, into two broad factions, with the sect master holding the balance.

The **Expansionist faction** is led by **Elder Rong Zhaoyuan (容昭遠)**, Foundation Establishment, age one hundred and eighty, head of the Merit Hall, a tall, soft-spoken man with a long beard and a smile that does not reach his eyes. The Expansionists' position: the Azure Sword Sect is the regional hegemon; the Cangli Riverlands' vein system is, geologically, the sect's; the Cangwu Sect is a dying sect whose resources are wasted on it; the formal claim should be filed, the dispute won, and the vein tapped. The Expansionists control the Merit Hall (which manages sect missions and grotto-heaven rotations — i.e., the sect's patronage system) and the Intelligence Hall (which produces the vein maps). Rong Zhaoyuan is Su Wenli's correspondent (§1.3); the correspondence is the Expansionists' back channel into the Cangwu Sect's internal politics.

The **Isolationist faction** is led by **Elder Jing You (靜幽)**, Foundation Establishment, age two hundred and ten, head of the Scripture Hall, a small, thin, elderly woman who looks one hundred and fifty and is, by reputation, the sect's best teacher since the founder. The Isolationists' position: the expansion risks war with the Jade Void Holy Land (which has historic interest in the Cangli Riverlands — the holy land's founder, two thousand years ago, was born in the Cangli region, and the holy land considers the region a spiritual ancestor-territory); the expansion risks the protagonist (already a Foundation Establishment cultivator with Old Chen's connections and, through Old Chen, the Jade Void's goodwill); the sect's resources are better spent on Leng Wushuang's third Nascent Soul attempt than on territorial acquisition. The Isolationists control the Scripture Hall (which controls the sect's practice tradition and inner-disciple training) and have the quiet sympathy of the Sword-Craft Hall and the Formation Hall (whose heads consider the expansion a distraction from craft).

Leng Wushuang holds the balance. Her personal position is expansionist — the vein is the resource for her third attempt — but she is wary of the Jade Void (correctly; the Jade Void's intelligence network is real and the holy land's interest in the Cangli region is documented) and she is wary of her own daughter's dissent. She has not committed. The commitment, when it comes (session ~300, with the formal claim), is the political turn that begins the Act 2 endgame.

### 2.6 Leng Qingxue (冷清雪): position, dissent, arc

Leng Qingxue is one hundred and twenty years old, looks thirty, and is the youngest Core Formation elder in the Azure Sword Sect's history. She is the head of the Punishment Hall (刑堂堂主) — a factional balancing appointment by Leng Wushuang, who gave her daughter real authority (the Punishment Hall enforces the sect's rules and adjudicates internal disputes) specifically to keep the Expansionist-controlled Merit and Intelligence Halls from controlling enforcement. Leng Qingxue is, in this sense, her mother's creature — placed where she is to serve her mother's balance strategy. She is also, in another sense, her mother's opposite.

Her reason for dissenting is older than her position. At age ninety (before her Core Formation breakthrough, when she was a Foundation Establishment inner disciple on grotto-heaven rotation), she was sent by the previous Punishment Hall head to investigate a complaint from a Cangwu Sect tenant village about an Azure Sword Sect outer disciple who had beaten a farmer over a grain dispute. Leng Qingxue investigated, found the complaint valid, and punished the outer disciple (a demerit and a fine). She also, in the course of the investigation, spent three weeks at the Cangwu Sect as Wu Changqing's guest (Wu Changqing was forty-two at the time, three years into his sect master-ship). The three weeks shaped her. She found the Cangwu Sect's water-phase tradition elegant (she had been trained in metal-phase; water-phase was, to her, a revelation of softness). She found Wu Changqing's grief and discipline moving. She found the vassalage arrangement, which she had not previously understood in concrete terms, morally indefensible. She returned to Biluo Peak and argued, in the elder council, against the tribute's current rate. The argument was overruled (the council, then as now, was Expansionist-led); Leng Qingxue accepted the overruling but did not change her view. Her Core Formation breakthrough, three years later, was achieved with a quiet conviction that the sect she was rising to serve was, in one respect, wrong.

Her arc (per doc 26 §9.3): the principled dissident within the sect (Act 2), the protagonist's political opposite and romance (Act 2-3), the exile who co-founds the Wang lineage school (Act 3, ending 1). Her arc is the arc of principle: she must choose, in Act 3, between her sect (her mother, her lineage, her duty, her Core Formation seat) and her principles (the Cangli Riverlands, the Cangwu Sect's legitimate claim, the protagonist, the law). She chooses principle. The choice costs her the sect. She does not, in the end, regret it — but the cost is real, and the simulation tracks it.

### 2.7 The three spirit veins

The Azure Sword Sect's three spirit veins are all metal-phase primary (a geological consequence of the Northern Cangwu range's granitic substrate, which is metal-phase-bearing; doc 14 §1.1) with varying secondary phases. They are, in descending order of output:

The **Biluo Vein** (碧螺脈) is the sect's primary vein, breaking the surface inside the compound walls beneath the Sword-Ancestor Hall. Its current runs deep (approximately four hundred meters below the peak's summit) and its output is sufficient to sustain two hundred cultivators at full Qi Condensation practice, or thirty at Foundation Establishment. The vein's surface trace is the **Founder's Spring** (祖師泉), a small spring in the Founder's Garden whose water is faintly metallic to the taste and which the sect's inner disciples drink from each morning as a minor cultivation aid. The Biluo Vein has been tapped for 180 years and shows no sign of thinning; its flow is stable, its channel wide. This is the vein that feeds the compound's main cultivation hall and the grotto-heaven's anchor (the grotto-heaven encloses the vein's main current; the surface trace is the anchor — per doc 24 §2.7).

The **North Cloud Vein** (北雲脈) is the sect's secondary vein, breaking the surface two li north of the compound, on the far side of Biluo Peak. Its current is shallower (one hundred and fifty meters) and its output is roughly half the Biluo Vein's. The vein's surface trace is a cold, mineral-heavy spring in a small hanging valley that the sect has developed into the **North Cloud Garden** (北雲苑), a terraced herb garden growing metal-phase and water-phase spirit herbs for the Pill Hall. The garden is managed by a Foundation Establishment elder (currently Elder Qi Shoujing, an Isolationist-leaning quiet ally of Jing You) and staffed by six inner disciples on rotating assignment. The North Cloud Vein's output is not directly tapped for cultivation; it feeds the herb garden, which feeds the Pill Hall, which produces the sect's pills. The chain is the sect's pharmaceutical backbone.

The **Iron Root Vein** (鐵根脈) is the sect's tertiary vein, breaking the surface four li southwest of the compound, in a wild region of the peak's lower slopes. Its current is the shallowest of the three (sixty meters) and its output is the smallest (sufficient for thirty Qi Condensation cultivators). The vein's surface trace is a stand of iron-root pine (the same species that grows in the grotto-heaven, doc 2.3 — the surface trace and the grotto-heaven population share a common ancestor population isolated when the grotto-heaven's boundary formed). The Iron Root Vein is the sect's sword-craft resource: the iron-root pine's wood, saturated by the vein's metal-phase qi, is dense enough to be forged into practice swords and, with sufficient treatment, into low-grade artifact-grade weapons. The Sword-Craft Hall manages the stand and the forge.

The three veins together produce enough qi to sustain the sect's four hundred disciples at Qi Condensation practice and approximately forty at Foundation Establishment practice — but not enough to fund a Nascent Soul breakthrough. Leng Wushuang's two failed attempts depleted the sect's spirit-stone reserves (the sect held, before the first attempt, a reserve of three thousand stones; the reserve is now four hundred). The third attempt requires, by Leng Wushuang's estimate, two thousand stones of concentrated qi and six months of uninterrupted grotto-heaven access. The Cangli Riverlands' deep vein extension (§2.4) is, for Leng Wushuang, the resource that closes this gap — either by direct tapping (if the formal claim succeeds) or by trade (if the Jade Void Holy Land can be induced to supply stones in exchange for territorial concessions).

### 2.8 The grotto-heaven rotation system

The Jade Edge Grotto's six-month rotations are the Azure Sword Sect's most contested internal resource. The system: every inner disciple is eligible for a six-month rotation once per three-year cycle; the rotation places them inside the grotto-heaven for intensive cultivation, with access to the grotto's metal-phase-accelerated qi, the herb population, and (under supervision) the beast population for combat practice. Three hundred and seventy of the four hundred disciples cycle through; the remaining thirty (the sect master, the three peak masters, the eight hall heads, and senior inner disciples on sect duty) practice in the compound's main cultivation hall, which is fed by a direct tap from the Biluo Vein.

Rotation slots are allocated by the Merit Hall (Expansionist-controlled, §2.5) on the basis of merit-points accumulated through sect missions, grotto-heaven herb-gathering, and (in practice) factional alignment. A disciple in good standing with Elder Rong Zhaoyuan's faction can expect a slot every three years; a disciple out of favor may wait five. The protagonist, on first contact with the Azure Sword Sect (session ~200, doc 26 §4.2), is offered a courtesy slot by Leng Wushuang — a political gesture (the protagonist is not a sect member and would not normally be offered grotto-heaven access) that the Expansionists receive as provocation and the Isolationists receive as a sign that Leng Wushuang is, despite her expansionist position, willing to extend the sect's courtesies to a powerful outsider. The protagonist's acceptance or refusal of the slot is the first Azure Sword Sect political choice and shapes the factional response to the protagonist for the rest of Act 2.

The grotto-heaven's rotation system has, over the sect's 180-year history, produced one structural problem: the grotto's ecology is not infinite. The sword-grass stands, the blue-flame lotus beds, and the iron-root pine groves are renewable but only at a rate set by the grotto's qi-climate; over-harvesting depletes them. The herb population has thinned measurably in the last sixty years (Jing You's Scripture Hall has documented this; Rong Zhaoyuan's Merit Hall has not acknowledged it). The silver-furred fox population is also down (the Isolationists attribute this to over-hunting by rotation disciples; the Expansionists attribute it to a natural cycle). The jade-scaled serpent, by all observations, is unaffected — but no one in the sect is willing to test whether that would change. The grotto's slow ecological decline is, for the Isolationists, an argument against expansion (the sect cannot manage what it has); for the Expansionists, an argument for it (the sect needs a second grotto-heaven to relieve the first).

### 2.9 The regional position: the Jade Void, the Alliance, and the courts

The Azure Sword Sect is the regional hegemon of the central and northern Cangwu range, but it is not the regional hegemon of the broader Cangli watershed. That position belongs, in cultivation-world accounting, to the **Jade Void Holy Land** (玉虛聖地, doc 24 §4.3) — a Nascent Soul+ holy land on another continent, with three thousand disciples and a peak grotto-heaven, whose founder (Daoist Jade Void, two thousand one hundred years old) was, per the holy land's own records, born in the Cangli Riverlands. The Jade Void's interest in the Cangli region is, formally, ancestral veneration — the holy land maintains a small shrine (staffed by two Foundation Establishment elders on rotating assignment) at the site of the founder's birth village, which is, by the holy land's archives' best estimate, somewhere in the central Cangwu foothills within thirty li of Wang Family Bend. The shrine's exact location has been lost (the founder's birth village was abandoned in a flood fourteen hundred years ago and the site has shifted); the Jade Void's rotating elders search for it, quietly, on their assignments, and have not found it. The Azure Sword Sect knows of this search and tolerates it, because the Jade Void's interest is, on balance, a strategic asset — a holy land's claim to the region deters other holy lands from contesting it.

The Azure Sword Sect's relationship to the Jade Void is therefore careful. The sect pays nominal homage to the holy land (a small tribute of one mid-grade spirit stone per decade, delivered by a Foundation Establishment elder to the Jade Void's rotating shrine staff); the Jade Void, in return, extends the sect its implicit protection against other holy lands. The arrangement is unwritten and is, in the cultivation world's accounting, fragile. If the Azure Sword Sect's expansion into the Cangli Riverlands is perceived by the Jade Void as a violation of the region's ancestral-veneration status, the holy land's protection could withdraw — and a holy land's withdrawn protection is, in the cultivation world's brutal accounting, an invitation to other powers to contest the now-unprotected region. This is the Isolationist faction's strongest argument (§2.5): the expansion risks the Jade Void's goodwill, and the Jade Void's goodwill is the sect's long-term strategic anchor.

The Azure Sword Sect's relationship to the **Immortal Alliance** (仙盟, doc 12 §6, doc 20 §1.3) is formal. The sect holds a mid-tier alliance seat (one of thirty in the eastern region), pays annual alliance dues (twelve mid-grade spirit stones), and is entitled to alliance adjudication of disputes with other member sects. The Cangli spirit vein dispute (§2.4), when it is filed at session ~300, will be filed with the alliance. The alliance's adjudication is slow (the average dispute resolution takes eight years) and the alliance's enforcement is weak (the alliance has no standing army; it relies on collective action by member sects). The Azure Sword Sect knows this; the Cangwu Sect knows this; both know that the formal adjudication is, in practice, a stalling tactic while the two sects maneuver on the ground. The protagonist's intervention (mediation, alliance lobbying, or — if the player chooses — bringing in the Jade Void as a third party) is the Act 2 political verb.

The Azure Sword Sect's relationship to the **Courts of Heaven** (天庭, doc 15, doc 20 §1.3) is distant. The courts have no direct jurisdiction over Acquired-stratum sect disputes; the courts' concern is records of the dead and heaven-sealed oaths. The Azure Sword Sect, however, has one courts-related obligation: the sect's Foundation Establishment+ cultivators, when they die, are subject to the courts' soul-rites (per doc 25 §5). The sect maintains a small temple (the **Sword-Ancestor Side-Shrine**, a single-room building near the Founder's Garden) staffed by a Qi Condensation lay cultivator who performs the basic soul-rites for the sect's dead. The shrine is not a temple in the institutional sense (doc 12 §4); it is a side-function the sect performs for its own. The courts know of the shrine and tolerate it; the courts do not, in practice, intervene in the sect's funerary practice as long as the basic rites are performed. This distant relationship is the sect's only direct contact with the Precelestial, and it is, for the sect's cultivators, an abstract concern — the courts are a thing one deals with at death, not in life. The protagonist, at Foundation Establishment, will deal with the courts directly only if and when a sect member's anchor requires formal processing (the death of Old Chen, doc 26 §12, is the first such event the protagonist navigates).

## Part 3 — The Eight Hexagrams

### 3.1 What the hexagrams are and how Wang Lun casts them

Wang Lun (王倫, 41, the lineage school teacher, doc 06 Scene 1) casts the hexagram by the yarrow-stalk method — the slow, careful procedure (fifty stalks, divided and counted in eighteen passes, producing six lines from bottom to top, each line solid or broken, each line possibly changing) that yields one of the sixty-four hexagrams of the Zhou Book of Changes (周易). The cast is deterministic from `seed + playerState` (doc 06 Scene 1, Procedural implications): the same seed and the same player state produce the same hexagram. The player state, at Scene 1 (autumn of the player's sixteenth year), is determined by the player's choices over the first five sessions — primarily the player's responses to the first two qi perceptions, the player's dialogue with Wang Lun during lessons, and the player's behavior in the lineage hall (whether they have hidden the perception, spoken of it, grieved in it, etc.). The state is one of eight profiles. Each profile produces one hexagram. The hexagram, in Wang Lun's reading, names what the player is becoming; the player's journal records what they heard.

The eight specified below are the eight most likely. (The other fifty-six hexagrams are reachable in principle but require player states that the first five sessions make unlikely — states of profound depression, ecstatic mania, or already-formed cultivation intent that the sixteen-year-old mortal protagonist is not yet capable of.) Each entry gives: the hexagram's name (Hanzi), the judgment text (the cryptic line Wang Lun reads aloud from the Book of Changes, in the traditional form), how the hexagram points toward Old Chen (the direction Wang Lun gives), and the journal entry the protagonist writes that night.

### 3.2 Hexagram 1: Zhun (屯) — Initial Difficulty

**Player state:** open and curious; the protagonist has perceived qi two or three times, has not hidden it, has spoken of it to no one, and approaches the perception with attention rather than fear.

**Judgment text (屯: 元亨, 利貞. 勿用有攸往, 利建侯):** "Initial difficulty. Supreme success. Perseverance furthers. Do not undertake anything yet; it furthers to install helpers."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the line "it furthers to install helpers" and pauses. He says, slowly, that the hexagram names a beginning — a difficult one — and that beginnings of this kind are not undertaken alone. He says he knows of one helper. He does not name Old Chen yet; he asks the protagonist whether they would accept a helper if one could be found. (The protagonist's answer here does not change the direction; Wang Lun gives the direction regardless. But the protagonist's answer changes the wording of the introduction.)

**Journal entry (written that night, in the protagonist's childish hand):** "Teacher Wang cast a hexagram for me. Zhun. Initial difficulty. He said beginnings like this are hard and you don't do them alone. He said he knew a helper. He asked if I would accept one. I said yes. He wrote a letter and sealed it and gave it to me. He said to take it to the hermit on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east. He said the hermit's name is Old Chen. He said Old Chen might not teach me. He said to go anyway."

### 3.3 Hexagram 2: Meng (蒙) — Youthful Folly

**Player state:** open but impatient; the protagonist has perceived qi, finds it exciting, has begun trying (clumsily, without instruction) to make it happen again, and is frustrated that it will not come on command.

**Judgment text (蒙: 亨. 匪我求童蒙, 童蒙求我. 初筮告, 再三瀆, 瀆則不告. 利貞):** "Youthful folly. Success. I do not seek the young fool; the young fool seeks me. The first casting tells; a second and third are confusion, and confusion tells nothing. Perseverance furthers."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the line "I do not seek the young fool; the young fool seeks me" and almost smiles. He says the hexagram is about the relationship between a teacher and a student, and the hexagram is clear: the teacher does not come to the student. He says there is a teacher, on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east, who will not come to the protagonist. He says the protagonist must go to him. He adds (reading the line about confusion) that the protagonist must not ask the same question twice; the first answer is the answer. He writes the letter.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Meng. Youthful folly. He said the teacher doesn't come to the student. He said I must go. He said don't ask twice. He gave me a letter for Old Chen, on the hill above Li Family Creek. I asked him if Old Chen would teach me. He said the hexagram says don't ask twice. I said I was only asking once. He said I had asked twice."

### 3.4 Hexagram 3: Kan (坎) — The Abysmal Water, doubled

**Player state:** resistant and afraid; the protagonist has perceived qi and the perception frightened them — the world gained a depth they did not want, and they have been trying not to perceive it again.

**Judgment text (坎: 習坎, 有孚, 維心亨, 行有尚):** "The Abysmal repeated. If you are sincere, you have success in your heart, and whatever you do succeeds."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the hexagram's name aloud — "Kan. The Abysmal. Water, over water. Danger, doubled." — and looks at the protagonist for a long moment. He says the hexagram names a state of being in danger and getting through it, not by escaping the danger but by going through it. He says the protagonist is in danger, though not the kind with a sword. He says there is a man on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east, who went through this danger once and survived. He says the man's name is Old Chen. He says Old Chen will understand. He writes the letter.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Kan. The Abysmal. Danger doubled. He looked at me a long time before he said anything. He said the hexagram is about going through danger, not around it. He said there is a man who went through it. Old Chen. On the hill above Li Family Creek. He said Old Chen would understand. I am afraid. The hexagram says to go through it anyway. I will go."

### 3.5 Hexagram 4: Gu (蠱) — What Has Been Spoiled

**Player state:** grieving; the protagonist has perceived qi and the perception has, in some way the protagonist cannot articulate, reminded them of Wang Xiaodi (the younger sibling dead two years) — perhaps the perception's quality of depth, or of presence-in-absence, has touched the unmourned grief.

**Judgment text (蠱: 元亨, 利涉大川. 先甲三日, 後甲三日):** "What has been spoiled. Supreme success. It furthers to cross the great water. Three days before the beginning, three days after."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the hexagram slowly. He sets down the yarrow stalks. He says, quietly, that the hexagram names decay — something that has been spoiled — and the work of repairing what has been spoiled. He says the work is long (the three days before and three days after) and that it crosses a great water. He says he knows a man who has done this work, on the hill above Li Family Creek. He says the man's name is Old Chen. He says Old Chen lost someone, once, and did not mourn them in time, and the not-mourning spoiled something in him, and he spent thirty years repairing it. He says the protagonist should go to Old Chen. He writes the letter. He does not mention Wang Xiaodi by name. The protagonist understands anyway.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Gu. What has been spoiled. He set down the stalks and he looked at me and he did not say Xiaodi's name. He said the hexagram is about decay and repair. He said there is a man who spoiled something in himself by not mourning and spent thirty years repairing it. He said to go to him. Old Chen. On the hill above Li Family Creek. I cried a little. Teacher Wang pretended not to see. He wrote the letter and gave it to me and said go."

### 3.6 Hexagram 5: Jin (晉) — Progress

**Player state:** ambitious; the protagonist has perceived qi, found it exhilarating, and is already (without words for it) imagining what they could do with this power — leave the village, become a cultivator, never carry water again.

**Judgment text (晉: 康侯用錫馬蕃庶, 晝日三接):** "Progress. The prince is given horses in great number, and in one day he is granted three audiences."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the hexagram and his expression tightens — not anger, but wariness. He says the hexagram names progress, and progress is real, and the hexagram is favorable. He says the hexagram also names a man who is given much in a short time, and that the protagonist should be careful of being given much too fast. He says there is a man, on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east, who broke himself by being given much too fast. He says the man's name is Old Chen. He says Old Chen will teach the protagonist to be slow, if the protagonist will let him. He writes the letter. He adds, as he seals it, that he hopes the protagonist will let him.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Jin. Progress. He said the hexagram is good but warned me about being given too much too fast. He said there is a man who broke himself that way. Old Chen. On the hill above Li Family Creek. He said Old Chen would teach me to be slow. I do not want to be slow. I want to be strong. But Teacher Wang sealed the letter and he said he hoped I would let Old Chen teach me to be slow, and the way he said it made me think being slow was not the same as being weak."

### 3.7 Hexagram 6: Ming Yi (明夷) — Darkening of the Light

**Player state:** doubting and disillusioned; the protagonist has perceived qi and the perception has not been a revelation but a burden — the world has more in it than the protagonist wanted, and the protagonist has been wishing, in the days since, that they had not perceived it.

**Judgment text (明夷: 利艱貞):** "Darkening of the light. In adversity it furthers to be persevering."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the hexagram and is silent for a long time. He says the hexagram names a light that has been wounded and hidden, not extinguished. He says the protagonist has perceived something true and is now wishing they had not, which is a kind of hiding of the light. He says there is a man on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east, who hid his own light for thirty years and is only now, in his old age, beginning to let it show again. He says the man's name is Old Chen. He says Old Chen will not force the protagonist to be bright. He says Old Chen will, if asked, sit with the protagonist in the dimness. He writes the letter.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Ming Yi. Darkening of the light. He said the hexagram is about a light that is wounded, not gone. He said I am hiding my light. He said there is a man who hid his light for thirty years. Old Chen. On the hill above Li Family Creek. He said Old Chen would sit with me in the dimness. I cried. Not a lot. Some. Teacher Wang did not pretend not to see this time. He handed me the letter and he put his hand on my head for a moment and then he took it away."

### 3.8 Hexagram 7: Bo (剝) — Peeling Away

**Player state:** content and stagnant; the protagonist has perceived qi, found it interesting, but has no particular urgency about pursuing it. They are willing to let the perception fade if it fades. They are, in the slow way of a sixteen-year-old mortal in Wang Family Bend, waiting for nothing in particular.

**Judgment text (剝: 不利有攸往):** "Peeling away. It does not further to go anywhere."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the hexagram and frowns. He says the hexagram names a stripping-away — a time when what is inessential falls off and what is essential remains. He says the hexagram does not favor going anywhere, which is a problem, because the protagonist should go somewhere. He says, after a pause, that the hexagram may be saying the protagonist should not go anywhere yet — that the protagonist should be still and let the inessential peel away until what is essential is visible. He says there is a man, on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east, who has been still for thirty years and has, in the stillness, found what is essential. He says the man's name is Old Chen. He says the protagonist should go to Old Chen, but slowly, and only after the inessential has peeled. He writes the letter. He says the protagonist can wait a week, or a month, before going.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Bo. Peeling away. He said the hexagram says don't go anywhere, which he said was a problem because I should go somewhere. He said I should wait, and let the inessential fall off, and then go to Old Chen on the hill above Li Family Creek. He said I could wait a week or a month. He wrote the letter and gave it to me. I think I will wait. The hexagram says wait. I do not know what is inessential yet. Maybe waiting will show me."

### 3.9 Hexagram 8: Bi (比) — Holding Together

**Player state:** lonely and seeking connection; the protagonist has perceived qi, and the perception has, in its quality of the-world-beneath-the-world, made the protagonist feel how alone they are in perceiving it. The lineage hall is full of people who do not perceive what the protagonist perceives. The protagonist wants someone who does.

**Judgment text (比: 吉. 原筮, 元永貞, 无咎. 不寧方來, 後夫凶):** "Holding together. Good fortune. Inquire of the oracle again. Supreme, eternal perseverance brings no blame. Those who are uncertain gradually join. Those who come too late meet misfortune."

**How it points to Old Chen:** Wang Lun reads the hexagram and his face softens. He says the hexagram names union — the holding-together of people who belong together — and that the hexagram is good. He says the protagonist is lonely, which is not a question. He says the lineage hall cannot hold the protagonist for long, because the protagonist's perception is not a lineage-hall perception. He says there is a man, on the hill above Li Family Creek, four li east, who perceives as the protagonist perceives, and who has been alone with it for thirty years and would, perhaps, no longer mind the company. He says the man's name is Old Chen. He writes the letter. He adds, as he hands it over, that the hexagram says those who come too late meet misfortune, and that the protagonist should not wait too long.

**Journal entry:** "Teacher Wang cast Bi. Holding together. He said the hexagram is about union. He said I am lonely. He said it like he was telling me the weather. He said there is a man who perceives as I perceive, on the hill above Li Family Creek, and that the man has been alone with it for thirty years. Old Chen. He said Old Chen would perhaps not mind the company. He said the hexagram says don't come too late. I will not come too late. I will go tomorrow."

### 3.10 On the other fifty-six hexagrams

The eight specified above are the eight most likely at Scene 1, given the player states the first five sessions can produce. The other fifty-six hexagrams of the Zhou Book of Changes are reachable in principle — the deterministic cast from `seed + playerState` can, for unusual seeds or unusual player states, produce any of the sixty-four — but each requires a player state that the sixteen-year-old mortal protagonist is structurally unlikely to occupy at session ~5. A brief accounting of the categories the simulation must handle (in case a seed produces them) and how Wang Lun handles each:

**The thirty-six hexagrams of stable, formed states** (Qian 乾, Kun 坤, Tai 泰, Pi 否, et al.) — these name conditions of cosmic equilibrium or its absence. The sixteen-year-old mortal protagonist has not lived long enough to occupy a stable equilibrium; Wang Lun, receiving one of these, would read it as a hexagram about the world's larger motion rather than the protagonist's, and would redirect the reading toward the smaller, more personal hexagram nested inside it (the nuclear hexagram, the hu gua 互卦). The redirection is a real divinatory technique; Wang Lun knows it; the simulation implements it.

**The twelve hexagrams of advanced cultivation states** (Guai 夬, Gou 姤, Bo 剝, Fu 復 — the twelve消息 hexagrams that describe the annual cycle of qi rising and falling) — these name realm-level transitions the protagonist has not yet approached. Wang Lun, receiving one, would read it as a forecast of the protagonist's long cultivation arc rather than a present-state description, and would (correctly) treat it as beyond his ability to interpret fully. He would say, in this case, "I cannot read this hexagram for you. It is too large. Go to Old Chen; he will know what to do with it." This is the only player-state category in which Wang Lun explicitly defers to Old Chen by name in the casting itself, rather than in the direction-giving after.

**The sixteen hexagrams of extreme emotional states** (Dui 兌 in its extreme forms, Zhen 震 in its extreme forms, et al.) — these name conditions of overwhelming joy, rage, terror, or grief that the protagonist could, in principle, occupy at session ~5 (a protagonist who has just learned of Wang Lun's impending pneumonia, a protagonist consumed by rage at Wang Zongde, a protagonist in the grip of unmourned Wang Xiaodi grief so acute it has produced a qi-deviation onset). These are reachable in principle but require specific player choices that the first five sessions make available only at the extremes. Wang Lun, receiving one, would read it carefully and would name what he sees — the protagonist's extremity — and would point to Old Chen with greater urgency than the eight common hexagrams warrant. The protagonist's journal entry, in these cases, is shorter (the protagonist is in no state to write at length) and more fragmentary.

The simulation must, for completeness, produce Wang Lun's reading for all sixty-four hexagrams (the eight specified in detail in §3.2-§3.9, plus the fifty-six accounted for here in summary). The fifty-six are not specified in this document at the same depth as the eight because they are, by the procedural contract, unlikely at session ~5 — but the simulation must handle them gracefully, with Wang Lun's reading and the protagonist's journal entry produced by a template (the eight specified entries provide the template's calibration set). The template is: name the hexagram, read the judgment text, name the player state the hexagram describes, point to Old Chen (with urgency proportional to the player state's extremity), write the letter. The eight specified entries are the template's eight calibration points; the fifty-six are the template's interpolated outputs.

### 3.11 Procedural and rendering implications

The hexagram cast is, per doc 06 Scene 1, deterministic from `seed + playerState`. The implementation contract: the engine's dialogue plugin (per doc 22) consumes the player-state vector produced by the first five sessions (a vector of approximately twelve dimensions, including perception-count, perception-attitude, dialogue-tone with Wang Lun, lineage-hall behavior, grief-state, ambition-state, fear-state, loneliness-state, contentment-state, doubt-state, reverence-state, and a residual state for outliers), hashes it together with the world seed, and selects one of the sixty-four hexagrams by a deterministic mapping. The mapping is biassed toward the eight specified in §3.2-§3.9 — the eight player-state vectors that the first five sessions can produce map directly to the eight hexagrams; the fifty-six summary hexagrams require either unusual seeds or player-state vectors at the extremes of the twelve dimensions.

The rendering of the cast (per doc 06 Scene 1, Rendering implications): the yarrow-stalk procedure is rendered accurately. Wang Lun's animation begins with the stalks spread on the low table; the player watches (or, if impatient, can skip — but the skip is regrettable, because the procedure is the scene's pacing and skipping it makes Wang Lun's reading feel rushed, which is the wrong feeling). Each of the eighteen passes is rendered: Wang Lun divides the stalks with his right hand, counts the left bundle in fours, sets the remainder aside, repeats. The six lines build from bottom to top on a slip of paper, each line drawn with a brushstroke (solid for yang, broken for yin), each line marked with a small dot if it is a changing line. The completed hexagram is held up; Wang Lun reads the judgment text aloud; the text appears in the journal as the protagonist writes the entry that night.

The journal entry (per doc 06 Scene 1, Aftermath) is a persistent object in the player's inventory — a small notebook with the hexagram's name, the judgment text, Wang Lun's reading, and the protagonist's own words. The protagonist can consult the journal at any point in the game. The journal's text does not change; what changes is the protagonist's understanding of it. A player who reads the journal at session ~5 (the night of the cast) reads one meaning; the same player reading the same journal at session ~450 (the night of Old Chen's death) reads a different meaning, because the protagonist has, by then, lived into the hexagram. This is the design intent: the hexagram is a fixed text the protagonist grows into, not a fixed prediction the protagonist fulfills. Wang Lun's divination (per doc 24 §2.5) perceives the qi-topography's current momentum, not the future; the hexagram names the momentum, and the protagonist's life is what the momentum becomes under the protagonist's free action. The journal, persistent and unchanged, is the textual record of this: a fixed cast, a living reading.

The hexagram also affects Old Chen's reception of the protagonist (per doc 06 Scene 2). Old Chen, on receiving the protagonist, asks (if the protagonist offers the letter) what hexagram Wang Lun cast. The protagonist tells him. Old Chen's response varies by hexagram: for Zhun (Initial Difficulty), Old Chen nods slowly and says "Good. A difficult beginning is the only kind worth having"; for Meng (Youthful Folly), Old Chen says "Good. The teacher does not come to the student. You came. That is the first lesson"; for Kan (The Abysmal), Old Chen is quiet for a long moment and then says "I know that hexagram. I cast it for myself, sixty years ago. Come in"; for Gu (What Has Been Spoiled), Old Chen says nothing for a long time and then says "I am sorry. Wang Lun should not have — but he was right to. Come in"; for Jin (Progress), Old Chen frowns and says "Be careful of that hexagram. It is the one I cast before my own failed breakthrough. Come in, and we will be slow"; for Ming Yi (Darkening of the Light), Old Chen says softly "Yes. I know. Come in. There is tea"; for Bo (Peeling Away), Old Chen says "Then you have come at the right time. Come in"; for Bi (Holding Together), Old Chen says, with a small and unexpected smile, "Good. I have been alone with it for thirty years. Come in." The eight responses are Old Chen's first teaching, delivered before the fraudulent-manual test (doc 06 Scene 2). They are the protagonist's first perception that Old Chen is not a hermit by temperament but a hermit by circumstance — a man who has been waiting, in a way he would not have admitted to anyone, for company.

## 5. Reviewer voices (per the doctrine: "Exhibit reviewer voices; do not self-certify")

**Reviewer A (the genre traditionalist):** "The Cangwu Sect is too sympathetic and the Azure Sword Sect is too nuanced. The genre wants clearly evil great sects and clearly virtuous small sects. You have given Wu Changqing a notebook and Leng Wushuang a dead husband and a third attempt, and now neither side is a villain. The player will not know whom to root for."

**Response:** The genre's clearly-evil-great-sect convention is the power-fantasy version of xianxia, in which the protagonist's rise is validated by the obvious villainy of those above them. This game's thesis (doc 26 §0, doc 05 §4.3) is that cultivation is becoming less limited, not becoming more powerful than the villains. The political engine of Act 2 requires that the Azure Sword Sect's expansion be lawful (if ruthless) and the Cangwu Sect's resistance be understandable (if doomed), because the protagonist's choice — defend the small sect, mediate, or step aside — is only meaningful if both sides have a real case. Leng Wushuang is not a villain; she is a woman who has failed twice and is gambling on a third attempt, and the resources for the attempt require an expansion the small sect cannot survive. Wu Changqing is not a hero; he is a man who broke himself at twenty-one and has spent thirty years presiding over the slow decline of the sect he inherited, and his grief has curdled into a bitterness he writes about in a notebook no one reads. The player roots for the protagonist, who must navigate between them. This is the doctrine's "confront the central tension directly" (AGENTS.md Part 3): the tension is real, the sides are real, and the protagonist's choice is the engine.

**Reviewer B (the systems designer):** "Nine named disciples at the Cangwu Sect is too many for the simulation to track with the depth you specify. Each disciple has an arc, a faction, a relationship to the protagonist, and a death-or-transformation trajectory. Across Act 2 (sessions 120-500), the simulation must track all nine plus the protagonist plus the Azure Sword Sect's named characters plus the companions. This is a cast of thirty. The simulation will choke."

**Response:** The simulation does not track all nine at full depth at all times. Each disciple has a baseline state (realm, faction, relationship vector) updated per solar term, and a foreground-depth that activates only when the protagonist is in the disciple's presence or when the disciple is the protagonist's current mission-relevant NPC. The nine named disciples are the sect's load-bearing NPCs; the other twenty-one (the unnamed outer and inner disciples) are simulated as a cohort with aggregate state. The cast of thirty is the cast of named, foreground-depth characters across the entire Act 2-3 arc, not the cast of simultaneously-foreground characters in any single session. The precedent is Crusader Kings' character-density model (hundreds of named characters, of which a dozen are foreground at any time) and Baldur's Gate 3's companion model (six companions, of which three are in the party at any time). This is shippable; the depth is calibrated to the foreground/background distinction, not to uniform simulation.

**Reviewer C (the production lead):** "Eight hexagrams with full judgment text, direction-giving, journal entry, and Old Chen response, plus a template for the other fifty-six. This is a writing load you have not budgeted."

**Response:** The eight specified entries are written (this document). The fifty-six template outputs are not fully written; they are produced by the template (§3.10) from the eight calibration points, with the writing team filling in the template's gaps for the fifty-six in a single sprint after the eight are validated. The total writing load is eight full entries (done) plus fifty-six template interpolations (a sprint). The Old Chen responses (§3.11) are eight additional lines of dialogue, written (this document). The journal entries' persistence across the 1500-session arc (the design intent that the same journal text reads differently at session 5 and session 450) is a rendering and UX concern, not a writing concern — the text does not change; the player's reading context does. This is shippable in the Act 1 vertical slice (the eight hexagrams are Scene-1 content) with the fifty-six template interpolations deferred to the Act 2 expansion.
